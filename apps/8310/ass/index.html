Alternate Stable States and Phase Shifts

Understanding Non-linear Dynamics in Ecological Systems

Introduction to Equilibrium Concepts

Ecological systems can exist in multiple states under the same environmental conditions. This fundamental concept challenges linear thinking about ecosystem responses and has profound implications for conservation and management.

Stable Equilibrium

A state that, when perturbed, returns to its original condition. Like a ball in a valley that rolls back to the bottom after being displaced.

Unstable Equilibrium

A state that, when slightly perturbed, moves away permanently. Like a ball balanced on a hilltop that rolls down after any disturbance.

Types of Phase Shifts

Continuous Phase Shifts

Gradual, smooth transitions that follow environmental changes predictably.

System State = g(Environmental Driver)
where g is a continuous, monotonic function

Catastrophic Bifurcations

Sudden shifts occurring when environmental drivers cross critical thresholds.

dx/dt = r + x² - c
Critical point occurs when discriminant = 0

Basin Boundary Crossings

Transitions between stable states caused by perturbations that push the system across basin boundaries.

Hysteresis Effects

Hysteresis: Path-dependent behavior where the current state depends not only on current conditions but also on the history of how those conditions were reached.

Management Implication: Due to hysteresis, the effort required to restore a degraded system may be much greater than what was needed to prevent the degradation initially.

Resilience Framework

Resistance

The magnitude of disturbance required to cause a change in system state. Measured by the width of the stability basin.

Resistance = |Threshold - Current State|

Resilience

The rate at which a system returns to equilibrium after disturbance. Related to the steepness of the stability landscape.

Resilience ∝ |∂f/∂x| at equilibrium

Early Warning Indicators

Critical Slowing Down: As systems approach tipping points, they lose resilience and recover more slowly from perturbations, creating detectable warning signals.

Increasing Variance

Fluctuations around the equilibrium increase as the system loses stability.

Rising Autocorrelation

The system develops "memory" as recovery from perturbations slows down.

Spatial Correlation

In spatially extended systems, correlation length increases near tipping points.

Mathematical Framework

General Dynamical System

dx/dt = f(x, c, t)
where:
x = system state vector
c = environmental parameter vector
t = time
f = system dynamics function

Stability Analysis

Equilibrium points: f(x*, c) = 0
Stability determined by eigenvalues of Jacobian:
J = ∂f/∂x|x*
Stable if Re(λi) < 0 for all eigenvalues λi

Bifurcation Conditions

Saddle-node bifurcation occurs when:
f(x*, c*) = 0 and ∂f/∂x|x*,c* = 0
Critical parameter: c* where stability changes

Potential Function Approach

For gradient systems: dx/dt = -dU/dx
where U(x) is the potential function
Stable equilibria at local minima of U

Management and Conservation Implications

Prevention vs Restoration

Due to hysteresis effects, preventing degradation is often more feasible than restoration after a shift has occurred.

Early Warning Systems

Monitoring variance and correlation can provide advance warning of approaching tipping points.

Adaptive Management

Management strategies must account for non-linear responses and potential threshold effects.

Spatial Considerations

Local management actions may have non-local effects through spatial coupling and connectivity.

Time Scale Considerations: What appears as alternate stable states on short time scales may be transient dynamics on longer time scales. Ecosystems may be "living dangerously on borrowed time" during slow, unrecognized regime shifts.

Core Concepts: States, Drivers, and Basins

State variables (e.g., coral cover, algal cover) describe the system itself. Driver (control) variables (e.g., nutrients, grazing pressure, temperature) are external conditions we change or that change the system. A basin of attraction is the set of initial states that converge to the same equilibrium; separatrices are the boundaries between basins.

Alternate stable states arise when positive feedbacks reinforce a state and negative feedbacks are insufficient to restore the prior state.

Why Multistability Happens: Feedback Mechanisms

  • Resource–environment feedback: Habitat engineers (macrophytes, corals, biocrusts) modify conditions to favor themselves.
  • Predator–prey & behavior: Fear/refuge effects reduce grazing in some states, reinforcing them.
  • Allee effects: Below a critical density, populations decline (mate limitation, cooperation), enabling low-density stable states.
  • Priority effects: Early occupants change conditions to favor their own persistence, locking in composition.

Bifurcations at a Glance

Saddle-node (fold)

Creation/annihilation of a stable–unstable pair; classic hysteresis and alternate states.

Transcritical

Stability exchange between equilibria; often no hysteresis.

Pitchfork

Symmetry-breaking leading to two symmetric stable states.

Hopf

Equilibrium loses stability to a limit cycle (regime shift to oscillations, not a second fixed point).

How Tipping Happens: B-, N-, and R-Tipping

Bifurcation-induced (B-tipping): A driver crosses a true stability threshold; the equilibrium disappears or changes stability.

Noise-induced (N-tipping): Random shocks push the system across a basin boundary although equilibria remain locally stable.

Rate-induced (R-tipping): Drivers change too rapidly for the system to track the moving equilibrium, causing escape to another basin.

Stochasticity: Noise, Flickering, and Quasi-Potentials

Random disturbances can cause brief excursions or flips between states (flickering) even before a bifurcation. In noisy systems, a quasi-potential landscape summarizes escape likelihoods: deeper basins imply longer mean escape times.

Spatial Dimensions of Regime Shifts

When patches are coupled, regime shifts can spread as fronts, stall at gradients (front pinning), or remain mosaic. Near tipping points, spatial variance and correlation length typically increase (spatial early warnings). Connectivity can stabilize (rescue) or synchronize collapses.

Early-Warning Indicators: Strengths and Caveats

  • Return rate ↓ (slower recovery), variance ↑, lag-1 autocorrelation ↑.
  • Skewness (asymmetric excursions), DFA (long-memory), and multivariate indicators for coupled variables.
  • Spatial EWI: correlation length, patch size distributions (e.g., increasing Moran’s I).

Caveat: Trends can be confounded by nonstationary drivers, seasonality, or measurement error; use null models/surrogates and sufficient time series length.

How Do We Test for Alternate Stable States?

  1. Initial-condition tests: Hold drivers constant; demonstrate different equilibria from different starts.
  2. Hysteresis tests: Ramp a driver up and down; forward and backward paths differ.
  3. Model-based diagnostics: Cusp catastrophe regression, potential analysis, Bayesian model comparison vs single-state models.
  4. Recovery diagnostics: Slowing return rates and increased variance/autocorrelation approaching the threshold.

Canonical Case Studies

  • Shallow lakes: Nutrient–turbidity–macrophyte feedback → clear/turbid states; classic loading–unloading hysteresis with phosphorus.
  • Kelp systems: Predator control (otters) and grazing → kelp forests vs urchin barrens.
  • Savanna–forest: Tree–grass–fire feedback; rainfall and ignition shift basins and create mosaics.
  • Soil biocrusts: Crust stabilizes soil; disturbance leads to erosion and a bare, persistent state.
  • Gut microbiome: Positive feedback via metabolites/pH can lock in dysbiosis vs eubiosis.

Management When Hysteresis Matters

Prevention is cheaper than cure because reversing a shift often requires pushing drivers past the original threshold. Identify leverage points (e.g., increase grazing, reduce nutrients) that deepen the desired basin. Use pulse interventions to cross separatrices, followed by press maintenance to keep the system in the target basin. Estimate hysteresis width empirically to size interventions.

Common Misinterpretations

  • Apparent “jumps” from sparse/short data ≠ true hysteresis.
  • Different states under different drivers ≠ alternate stable states.
  • Oscillations/chaos can be regime shifts, but not necessarily multistability of fixed points.

Glossary

  • Alternate stable states: Two or more stable equilibria under the same drivers.
  • Basin of attraction: Set of initial conditions converging to the same equilibrium.
  • Hysteresis: Path dependence; forward and backward transitions at different driver levels.
  • Critical slowing down: Recovery rate declines near a tipping point.
  • Separatrix: Boundary between basins in state space.

Two-Dimensional View: Nullclines and Stability

In a plant–herbivore model with a saturating functional response and a refuge (or Allee effect), the plant nullcline can become non-monotonic, intersecting the herbivore nullcline three times—yielding two stable equilibria separated by an unstable one. Stability at each intersection follows from the Jacobian eigenvalues.
