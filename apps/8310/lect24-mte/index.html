3. Damuth's Rule and Energy Equivalence

Population density scales inversely with body size, creating energetic equivalence across species.

Damuth's Rule:

$$\text{Population Density} = aM^{-3/4}$$ $$\text{Energy Equivalence: } \rho \times R = aM^{-3/4} \times bM^{3/4} = \text{constant}$$

Population Density vs Body Mass

Key Implications:

  • Energy Equivalence: Populations use similar energy per unit area regardless of body size
  • Conservation Insight: Large-bodied species are naturally rare and thus vulnerable
  • Community Structure: Predictable biomass pyramids across ecosystems
  • Ecosystem Function: Size-structured energy flow through food webs

Original Study: Damuth (1981)

Analysis of 307 mammalian primary consumers revealed log D = -0.75 log W + 4.23 (r = -0.86), establishing the fundamental relationship between body size and abundance.

Damuth, J. (1981). Nature 290: 699-700

4. Temperature and the Arrhenius Equation

Temperature affects biological rates exponentially, following chemical kinetics principles.

Arrhenius Equation:

$$B = ae^{-E_a/kT}$$ $$\ln(B) = \ln(a) - \frac{E_a}{k} \cdot \frac{1}{T}$$

Where: B = biological rate, Ea = activation energy (~0.65 eV), k = Boltzmann constant, T = temperature (K)

$$B = B_0 M^\alpha e^{-E_a/kT}$$ $$\text{For biological times: } t = t_0 M^{1/4} e^{E_a/kT}$$

Universal biological clock: all organisms operate at similar rates when size- and temperature-corrected

7. Jensen's Inequality and Temperature Variability

For nonlinear functions, the average of the function ≠ function of the average. Critical for understanding thermal variability effects.

Jensen's Inequality:

$$E[f(X)] \neq f(E[X]) \text{ when } f(X) \text{ is nonlinear}$$ $$\text{For convex functions: } E[f(X)] > f(E[X])$$ $$\text{For concave functions: } E[f(X)] < f(E[X])$$

8. Limitations and Modern Perspectives

While MTE provides powerful insights, real biological systems show important deviations and context dependencies.

Scaling Deviations Across Groups

Known Limitations:

  • Scaling Variability: Exponents range from 0.67 to 1.0, not always 3/4
  • Taxonomic Differences: Different scaling relationships across major groups
  • Ecological Context: Environmental factors modify basic scaling patterns
  • Developmental Stages: Scaling changes with ontogeny
  • Activity Levels: Lifestyle affects metabolic scaling

Critical Analysis: Allen-Jacobson (2018)

PhD dissertation showing that metabolic scaling exponents vary significantly with social organization: solitary species ≈ 3/4, colonial species show intermediate values, aggregates ≈ 1.

Allen-Jacobson, C.F. (2018). PhD Dissertation, University of Florida
