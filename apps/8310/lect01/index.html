Course Overview

Population and community ecology form the foundation of understanding how organisms interact with their environment and with each other. This introductory lecture establishes the conceptual framework for the entire course, covering the scope, methods, and fundamental principles that govern ecological systems at the population and community levels.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the scope and scale of population and community ecology
  • Distinguish between population-level and community-level processes
  • Recognize the importance of spatial and temporal scales in ecological studies
  • Identify key methodological approaches in population and community ecology
  • Understand the historical development of ecological theory

The Ecological Hierarchy

Ecology operates across multiple levels of biological organization, each with distinct properties and processes:

Individual Level

Physiological ecology, behavior, life history traits, and adaptation to environmental conditions.

Population Level

Dynamics of single species including growth, regulation, genetics, and evolution within populations.

Community Level

Interactions among species including competition, predation, mutualism, and community structure.

Ecosystem Level

Energy flow, nutrient cycling, and interactions between biotic and abiotic components.

Population Ecology Fundamentals

Population ecology focuses on the dynamics of single species, examining how populations change in size, structure, and genetic composition over time and space.

Core Concepts in Population Ecology

Population Growth

Exponential and logistic growth models, carrying capacity, and growth rate parameters. Understanding how populations increase or decrease under different environmental conditions.

Population Regulation

Density-dependent and density-independent factors that control population size. Mechanisms of population regulation including competition and predation.

Life History Theory

Trade-offs between survival and reproduction, r- and K-selection, and optimization of life history strategies under different environmental conditions.

Spatial Population Dynamics

Metapopulation theory, source-sink dynamics, and the role of dispersal in population persistence and evolution.

Population Genetics

Allele frequencies, genetic drift, gene flow, and natural selection within populations. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and deviations from it.

Stochastic Processes

Environmental stochasticity, demographic stochasticity, and extinction risk in small populations. Population viability analysis.

Mathematical Modeling in Population Ecology

Population ecology relies heavily on mathematical models to predict and understand population dynamics. Key modeling approaches include:

Deterministic Models

  • Exponential growth: dN/dt = rN
  • Logistic growth: dN/dt = rN(1 - N/K)
  • Age-structured models using Leslie matrices
  • Stage-structured population models

Stochastic Models

  • Birth-death processes
  • Environmental stochasticity models
  • Demographic stochasticity models
  • Population viability analysis (PVA)

Community Ecology Fundamentals

Community ecology examines patterns and processes involving multiple interacting species within ecological communities.

Species Interactions

Competition

Interspecific and intraspecific competition for limited resources. Competitive exclusion principle, resource partitioning, and coexistence mechanisms.

Predation

Predator-prey dynamics, functional and numerical responses, and the role of predation in structuring communities.

Mutualism

Beneficial interactions between species including pollination, seed dispersal, and symbiotic relationships.

Parasitism

Host-parasite dynamics, effects on host populations, and the role of parasites in community structure.

Commensalism & Amensalism

Neutral and negative interactions where one species is unaffected by the interaction.

Facilitation

Positive interactions where one species enhances the performance of another without direct benefit.

Community Structure and Dynamics

Diversity Patterns

  • Species richness and evenness
  • Alpha, beta, and gamma diversity
  • Diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, etc.)
  • Species-area relationships
  • Latitudinal diversity gradients

Community Assembly

  • Ecological succession (primary and secondary)
  • Colonization and extinction dynamics
  • Priority effects and historical contingency
  • Neutral theory vs. niche theory

Food Webs and Trophic Structure

  • Food chains and food webs
  • Trophic levels and energy transfer
  • Top-down vs. bottom-up control
  • Keystone species and ecosystem engineers
  • Trophic cascades

Spatial Patterns in Communities

Key Spatial Concepts

  • Habitat heterogeneity and patch dynamics
  • Edge effects and fragmentation
  • Metacommunity theory
  • Dispersal limitation and mass effects
  • Species sorting and neutral assembly

Essential Definitions

The following terms represent fundamental concepts that every PhD student in population and community ecology should understand thoroughly:

Population
A group of individuals of the same species occupying a defined geographical area and potentially interbreeding.
Community
An assemblage of populations of different species living in the same place at the same time and potentially interacting.
Intrinsic Rate of Increase (r)
The maximum potential rate of population growth under ideal conditions, representing the difference between birth and death rates.
Carrying Capacity (K)
The maximum population size that an environment can sustain indefinitely given available resources and environmental conditions.
Density Dependence
Population regulation mechanisms where birth rates, death rates, or dispersal rates change as a function of population density.
Metapopulation
A population of populations - a set of local populations connected by dispersal, where local extinctions are balanced by recolonization.
Competitive Exclusion Principle
The principle stating that two species competing for the same limiting resource cannot coexist indefinitely; one will exclude the other.
Ecological Niche
The total range of environmental conditions under which a species can survive and reproduce, including all resources and habitat requirements.
Functional Response
The change in predation rate by an individual predator as prey density changes, typically categorized as Type I, II, or III responses.
Numerical Response
The change in predator population size in response to changes in prey population size, typically through reproduction or aggregation.
Species Diversity
A measure combining both species richness (number of species) and species evenness (relative abundance of species) in a community.
Trophic Cascade
Indirect effects that occur when predators suppress herbivore populations, thereby releasing plants from herbivory pressure.
Keystone Species
A species that has a disproportionately large effect on community structure relative to its abundance.
Source-Sink Dynamics
Population dynamics where high-quality habitats (sources) produce emigrants that support populations in low-quality habitats (sinks).
Life History Strategy
The evolved pattern of survival, growth, and reproduction that characterizes a species, including trade-offs between reproduction and survival.
r-Selection vs. K-Selection
Evolutionary strategies where r-selected species emphasize high reproductive rates and rapid development, while K-selected species emphasize competitive ability and survival.
Ecological Succession
The predictable sequence of changes in community composition following disturbance or colonization of new habitat.
Alpha, Beta, and Gamma Diversity
Local diversity within habitats (alpha), diversity between habitats (beta), and total regional diversity (gamma) across a landscape.
Environmental Stochasticity
Random fluctuations in environmental conditions that affect population growth rates and increase extinction risk.
Demographic Stochasticity
Random variation in individual survival and reproduction that affects population growth, particularly important in small populations.
Population Viability Analysis (PVA)
Quantitative assessment of extinction risk using demographic models that incorporate stochastic processes and environmental variation.
Neutral Theory
Theory proposing that community structure is determined primarily by random processes rather than species-specific differences in fitness.
Metacommunity
A set of local communities linked by dispersal of potentially interacting species across multiple patches.
Effective Population Size (Ne)
The number of individuals in an idealized population that would experience the same amount of genetic drift as the actual population being studied.
Allee Effect
A phenomenon where fitness decreases at low population densities, potentially leading to extinction vortices in small populations.
