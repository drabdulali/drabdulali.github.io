Endemic vs. Epidemic Disease

Definitions An epidemic is a transient surge of cases; an endemic disease maintains a non-zero equilibrium prevalence over time. A pathogen that invades does not necessarily persist—persistence requires that the influx of susceptibles balances removal (recovery, death, immunity).

At endemic equilibrium (SIR with demography):

$$\frac{dI}{dt}=0 \Rightarrow \beta S^* I^* - (\gamma + d)I^* = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{S^*}{N} = \frac{1}{R_0},\quad R_0=\frac{\beta N}{\gamma+d}.$$

Take-home: If \(S^*/N < 1/R_0\), transmission cannot sustain itself and prevalence decays; if \(S^*/N = 1/R_0\), endemic equilibrium is possible.

SIR with Demography & Virulence

Model with births and deaths

We add per-capita birth \(b\) and natural death \(d\) to the classic SIR, tracking total population \(N=S+I+R\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dS}{dt}&= bN - \beta SI - dS,\\ \frac{dI}{dt}&= \beta SI - \gamma I - dI,\\ \frac{dR}{dt}&= \gamma I - dR. \end{aligned}$$

With no virulence, population size follows \(\frac{dN}{dt} = (b-d)N\), i.e., exponential growth/decline unless \(b=d\).

Adding pathogen-induced mortality (virulence)

Let \(v\) be the additional per-capita mortality in infecteds:

$$\frac{dI}{dt}=\beta SI-(\gamma+d+v)I,\qquad R_0=\frac{\beta N}{\gamma+d+v}.$$

Virulence moves to the denominator: more virulent pathogens are harder to invade/persist, all else equal.

Estimating \(R_0\) from Serology (Using \(S^*/N = 1/R_0\))

At endemic equilibrium in SIR with demography, the susceptible fraction equals the inverse of \(R_0\): \(S^*/N=1/R_0\). If we can estimate the susceptible fraction from seroprevalence (\(\text{Immune}=1-S^*/N\)), we can back-calculate \(R_0\) and \(\beta\).

$$R_0 = \frac{1}{S^*/N},\qquad \beta = \frac{R_0(\gamma + d)}{N}.$$

Persistence Mechanisms, CCS & Seasonality

Critical Community Size (CCS)

CCS is the smallest population that can sustain transmission without frequent stochastic fade-outs. Small populations fall below \(S^*\) for longer after epidemics (few births), promoting extinction of infection; large populations replenish \(S\) rapidly and remain above CCS.

Seasonal Forcing

Seasonal contact patterns (e.g., school terms; rural–urban movements) produce multi-annual cycles. We model this by \(\beta(t)=\beta_0\,[1+a\cos(2\pi t/T)]\).

Case Study: Phocine Distemper Virus (PDV) in Seals

Observation. The 1988 North Sea PDV epidemic spread rapidly among harbor seals, with location-specific epidemics and a surprisingly long overall duration. Simple compartmental models predicted unrealistically large host numbers would be required for persistence (CCS \(\gtrsim 10^8\)), far greater than the global seal population (\(\sim 10^6\)).

Take-home: Spatial structure (patchy outbreaks) and reintroduction from a reservoir (e.g., harp seals) are plausible mechanisms extending apparent persistence, even when single-population CCS arguments say “no.”

Pathogen Impacts & Regulation of Hosts

Mechanisms

  • Increase mortality in \(I\) (virulence \(v\)), reducing \(N\) and growth rate \(r=b-d-v I/N\).
  • Reduce births indirectly (morbidity) or via density-dependent feedbacks as disease increases.
  • Regulate hosts: populations recover when small but are held in check when large.

With virulence: \(\frac{dN}{dt}=(b-d)N - vI.\) If \(vI\) offsets \((b-d)N\), exponential growth is curtailed—disease acts as a regulating force.

Take-home: The same processes that sustain infection can feed back to limit host abundance—regulation is a population-level outcome of pathogen transmission.

Case Study: House Finch Conjunctivitis (Mycoplasma gallisepticum)

Observation. A novel strain spilled over to house finches in the early 1990s, spreading rapidly with clear clinical signs and environmental/direct transmission. Three lines of evidence indicate host regulation: (1) ~50% density reduction, (2) stabilization to a threshold population size, and (3) greater losses where initial density was higher.

Take-home: Pathogen invasion led to a new regulated equilibrium: when finch numbers were low, they increased; when high, disease limited further growth—textbook density-dependent regulation via infection.

Summary & Key Insights

  • Endemicity requires replenishing susceptibles (demography, loss of immunity), connectivity (migration), or periodic boosts (seasonality) and may involve reservoirs.
  • SIR+Demography yields \(S^*/N=1/R_0\); virulence lowers \(R_0\) and can regulate host \(N\).
  • CCS explains why small populations show fade-outs; large populations show regular cycles with seasonality.
  • PDV underscores the role of space and reservoirs in persistence when single-population CCS says “no.”
  • House Finch demonstrates disease-driven regulation at landscape scale.
