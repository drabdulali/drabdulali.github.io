Introduction: Beyond Single Host-Pathogen Systems

SIMPLE to COMPLEX: Remember the progression

Interactions multiply with more hosts
Multi-host pathogens dominate reality

Traditionally, infectious disease ecology has focused on simple one host-one pathogen systems. Classic examples include seal-PDV (phocine distemper virus), human-measles, rabbit-myxomatosis, and Soay sheep-nematodes. While these systems have provided fundamental insights into disease dynamics, they represent an oversimplification of nature.

Key Insight: The reality is far more complex: approximately 60 percent of human pathogens and 80 percent of domestic animal pathogens can infect multiple host species. Multi-host pathogens are the norm, not the exception.

Community Epidemiology Framework

Community epidemiology examines multi-host and multi-parasite interactions within an ecological community context. This lecture focuses on three primary interaction types:

  • Multi-host parasites: One pathogen infecting multiple host species
  • Host-predator-prey dynamics: How predators influence disease in their prey
  • Coinfection: Multiple parasites within a single host (covered previously)
Visual Memory Anchor: Picture a web, not a chain. In single-host systems, we have a simple chain: Susceptible → Infected → Recovered. In multi-host systems, imagine a spider web where each node is a different species, and disease threads connect them in complex, overlapping patterns. One infected spider (host species) can send vibrations (pathogens) throughout the entire web, affecting species that never directly touch each other.

Case Study 1: The Great British Squirrel Replacement

The Invasion of Grey Squirrels and Squirrelpox Virus

Background: The red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) is native to Britain and has inhabited UK woodlands for millennia. In the late 19th century, grey squirrels (Sciurus carolinensis) were introduced from North America as ornamental species. What followed was one of the most dramatic examples of disease-mediated competitive exclusion in modern ecology.

The Pathogen: Squirrelpox Virus

  • Causes minimal disease in grey squirrels (native North American host)
  • Produces severe lesions, discharge, and scabs in red squirrels
  • Kills red squirrels rapidly within 5-7 days of infection
  • Serological evidence: 60 percent of grey squirrels have antibodies versus only 3 percent of red squirrels

Transmission Routes:

  • Direct contact: Through infected skin lesions during territorial disputes or mating
  • Vector-borne: Mosquitoes serve as passive mechanical vectors, transferring virus on mouthparts
  • Environmental: Virus persists on bird feeders and other fomites where both species feed

Observational Evidence: Long-term monitoring data (1960-1982) tracked the number of 5 km × 5 km grid squares occupied by each species across Britain. The data revealed a striking pattern: as grey squirrel range expanded, red squirrel range collapsed far faster than could be explained by resource competition alone.

Mathematical Modeling (Tompkins et al. 2002, 2003): Researchers developed two models:

  1. Resource competition only: Predicted gradual red squirrel decline over decades
  2. Resource competition + shared virus: Predicted rapid red squirrel collapse matching observed data

The model with both competition and disease accurately predicted the observed 25-fold acceleration in red squirrel decline.

Take-Home Message: This case demonstrates that a shared pathogen can act as a "biological weapon" in species interactions. Grey squirrels function as a disease reservoir—maintaining high pathogen prevalence with minimal fitness costs—while red squirrels suffer catastrophic mortality. The virus converts what might have been gradual competitive exclusion into rapid ecological replacement. This is apparent competition in action: the two species compete not for resources, but through their differential ability to tolerate a shared natural enemy.

Control Implications for Red Squirrel Conservation

Understanding the disease-mediated mechanism of red squirrel decline has important management implications:

  • Grey squirrel culling: Models suggest a 60 percent reduction in grey squirrel populations would be necessary to halt red squirrel decline—a logistically challenging target
  • Vaccination: Developing an oral vaccine for red squirrels could provide immunity without capturing individuals
  • Natural recovery: Field observations indicate occasional natural recovery from infection, suggesting some red squirrels possess resistance alleles
  • Biological control via predation: Pine martens (Martes martes) preferentially prey on grey squirrels because they are slower and spend more time on the ground. Areas with recovering pine marten populations have shown red squirrel population stability
Key Insight: The pine marten example reveals how predators can indirectly benefit one prey species by selectively removing another. This demonstrates that community epidemiology must consider not just hosts and pathogens, but the entire food web.

Apparent Competition: When Parasites Mediate Host Interactions

WEAPON: Understanding Apparent Competition
Weapon—shared parasites act as biological weapons
Enemy—hosts linked through a shared natural enemy
Additional mortality—one host amplifies parasite abundance
P* rule—species tolerating highest parasite load wins
Outcome—often extirpation of less tolerant host
No direct competition—hosts may not compete for resources

Defining Apparent Competition

Apparent competition is a negative interaction between two host species mediated by a shared natural enemy (parasite, pathogen, parasitoid, or predator). Unlike traditional resource competition where species directly compete for limiting resources (food, space, mates), apparent competition operates indirectly: the presence of one host species decreases the fitness of another species solely through its effect on the abundance of their shared enemy.

The P* Rule for Apparent Competition
The species that can tolerate the highest equilibrium parasite abundance (P*) will exclude other hosts through apparent competition
If Host 1's P*₁ > Host 2's P*₂, then Host 1 wins

Mechanism of Apparent Competition

Consider two host species (H₁ and H₂) sharing a parasite (P):

  1. Parasite amplification: H₂ supports high parasite reproduction, increasing environmental parasite load
  2. Spillover effect: Elevated parasite abundance from H₂ increases infection pressure on H₁
  3. Differential mortality: If H₁ suffers higher parasite-induced mortality than H₂, H₁ populations decline
  4. Positive feedback: As H₁ declines, H₂ may expand (less competition), further amplifying parasites
  5. Extirpation: H₁ may be locally eliminated even if it could persist with the parasite alone
Visual Memory Anchor: Imagine two castles (host species) under siege by an army (parasites). Castle A (grey squirrels) has thick walls and can withstand prolonged attack while its citizens continue reproducing soldiers for the enemy army. Castle B (red squirrels) has weak walls and rapidly crumbles under assault. The paradox: Castle A's resilience makes it a parasite factory, producing the very forces that overwhelm Castle B. Castle B would survive a siege alone, but Castle A's presence ensures Castle B's destruction.

Case Study 2: Experimental Demonstration with Parasitoid Wasps

Bonsall and Hassell's Moth-Parasitoid System

Experimental Design (Bonsall & Hassell 1998, Journal of Animal Ecology):

Researchers established population cages (30 cm × 30 cm × 30 cm) with three species:

  • Host 1: Ephestia kuehniella (flour moth)
  • Host 2: Plodia interpunctella (Indian meal moth)
  • Shared parasitoid: Venturia canescens (ichneumonid wasp)

The cage was divided by nylon mesh that allowed the tiny parasitoid to pass but restricted moth movement, ensuring controlled encounter rates.

Results:

  • Ephestia + Venturia alone: Both populations persisted in stable oscillations for 60+ weeks
  • Plodia + Venturia alone: Both populations persisted in stable oscillations for 60+ weeks
  • All three species together: Ephestia went extinct by week 40, while Plodia and Venturia persisted

Mechanism: Plodia supported higher parasitoid reproduction rates, creating an elevated parasitoid abundance that Ephestia could not tolerate. This demonstrates the P* rule: Plodia's higher tolerance for parasitoid attack allowed it to win apparent competition.

Take-Home Message: This elegant experiment proves that apparent competition is a real ecological force. Two host species that can each coexist with a parasitoid individually cannot necessarily coexist together. The species that amplifies parasitoid abundance more effectively (Plodia) drives the other to extinction. This has profound implications for biological control, invasive species management, and conservation biology.

What Makes a Multi-host Parasite?

Visual Memory Anchor: Think of parasites as locks and hosts as keys. Specialists are high-security locks requiring one perfect key. Generalists are master locks with simpler mechanisms that many keys can open. The question is: what makes a parasite willing to accept multiple keys?

The Conventional View: Parasites Should Specialize

Theory suggests parasites should evolve host specificity for several reasons:

  • Intimate association: Parasitism requires precise molecular interactions between parasite and host cells
  • Host defense evolution: Even within a species, not all host genotypes are susceptible (recall matching alleles and gene-for-gene models)
  • Host-specific machinery: Cellular receptors, immune responses, and physiological conditions vary among host species
  • Trade-off hypothesis: Selection for high performance in one host should reduce performance in others
  • Jack-of-all-trades, master of none: Generalists should be outcompeted by specialists
Key Insight: Despite theoretical expectations of specialization, empirical data reveal the opposite: 60 percent of human pathogens (n=1,415) and 80 percent of domestic animal pathogens (n=616) are multi-host parasites. Examples include Nipah virus, West Nile virus, Ebola, influenza, rinderpest, and Mycobacterium bovis.

Parasite Traits Facilitating Multi-host Transmission

VECTOR: Traits of Multi-host Pathogens
Vector-borne or environmental transmission
Evolutionary flexibility (high mutation)
Cross-species encounters enabled
Transmission modes that bypass host specificity
Often viral (short generation time)
Rapid adaptation to new hosts

1. Transmission Strategy

Transmission mode strongly predicts host range. Vector-borne and environmentally-transmitted pathogens show broader host ranges because:

  • Reduced host specificity requirements: Vectors (mosquitoes, ticks) mechanically transfer pathogens between species without requiring precise host-pathogen molecular matching
  • Environmental persistence: Pathogens with free-living stages (water-borne, fecal-oral) can infect any host encountering contaminated environments
  • Bridging species barriers: Vectors feeding on multiple host species create transmission networks spanning phylogenetically distant taxa

In contrast, sexually transmitted and vertically transmitted parasites show narrow host ranges due to intimate contact requirements and reproductive compatibility constraints.

2. Pathogen Type: The Viral Advantage

Viruses are overrepresented among multi-host pathogens due to:

  • Short generation times: Viral replication occurs in hours to days versus months for macroparasites
  • High mutation rates: RNA viruses especially lack proofreading, generating 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁶ mutations per nucleotide per replication
  • Large population sizes: Billions of virions produced per infection provide vast genetic variation
  • Rapid adaptation: The combination creates evolutionary potential to overcome new host defenses within single infections

Host Traits Facilitating Multi-host Sharing

1. Geographic Range Overlap

Species that overlap spatially share more parasites. This reflects:

  • Encounter opportunity: Co-occurrence increases cross-species contact rates
  • Shared vectors: Overlapping ranges mean shared mosquito, tick, and flea populations
  • Environmental contamination: Shared water sources, feeding sites, and territorial boundaries

2. Evolutionary Similarity (Phylogenetic Distance)

Phylogenetic Constraint on Host Range
Parasite sharing ∝ 1 / Phylogenetic Distance
Closely related species share more parasites than distant relatives

This pattern arises because related species share:

  • Ancestral parasites: Parasites inherited from common ancestors diversify with their hosts
  • Similar cellular machinery: Receptor molecules, cell surface proteins, and intracellular environments
  • Comparable immune responses: Related species employ similar immune molecules and pathways
  • Physiological compatibility: Body temperature, pH, and metabolic conditions

Davies and Pedersen (2008) demonstrated that mammal species within the same family share on average 5-10 times more parasite species than mammals from different orders. For example, red squirrels and grey squirrels (both genus Sciurus) readily share parapoxvirus, whereas transmission between squirrels and ungulates would be virtually impossible.

Mathematical Framework for Multi-host Systems

From Single Host to Multi-host Models

We begin with the familiar single-host SIS (Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible) framework and progressively add complexity.

Single Host SIS Model
dS/dt = -β S I
dI/dt = β S I - δ I
Where: β = transmission rate, δ = recovery or death rate

This model tracks susceptible (S) and infected (I) individuals within a single host species. Infection occurs at rate β through contact between S and I individuals, while infected individuals are removed at rate δ through recovery or disease-induced mortality.

Two-Host System with Isolated Transmission

The simplest multi-host model considers two host species with pathogen transmission only within species (no cross-species transmission):

Host Species 1
dS₁/dt = -β₁₁ S₁ I₁
dI₁/dt = β₁₁ S₁ I₁ - δ₁ I₁
Host Species 2
dS₂/dt = -β₂₂ S₂ I₂
dI₂/dt = β₂₂ S₂ I₂ - δ₂ I₂

Here β₁₁ and β₂₂ represent within-species transmission rates, while δ₁ and δ₂ capture species-specific mortality/recovery. At this stage, the two host populations are epidemiologically independent.

Two-Host System with Cross-Species Transmission

The critical feature of multi-host systems is cross-species transmission, represented by β₁₂ (transmission from species 2 to species 1) and β₂₁ (transmission from species 1 to species 2):

Host Species 1
dS₁/dt = -β₁₁ I₁ S₁ - β₂₁ I₂ S₁
dI₁/dt = β₁₁ I₁ S₁ + β₂₁ I₂ S₁ - δ₁ I₁
Host Species 2
dS₂/dt = -β₂₂ I₂ S₂ - β₁₂ I₁ S₂
dI₂/dt = β₂₂ I₂ S₂ + β₁₂ I₁ S₂ - δ₂ I₂
Key Insight: Notice that species 1's infection rate now includes two terms: within-species transmission (β₁₁ I₁ S₁) and cross-species transmission from species 2 (β₂₁ I₂ S₁). This coupling means the epidemiological dynamics of the two hosts are inextricably linked.

The WAIFW Matrix: Who Acquires Infection From Whom

WAIFW: The Transmission Contact Matrix
Who acquires infection
And from whom
Infection flows
From all sources
Within and among species

For systems with n host species, we organize transmission rates into a matrix W where element W_ij represents the transmission rate from species j to species i:

Transmission FROM
Transmission TO Species 1 Species 2
Species 1 β₁₁ (within) β₁₂ (among)
Species 2 β₂₁ (among) β₂₂ (within)

Diagonal elements (β₁₁, β₂₂) represent within-species transmission, while off-diagonal elements (β₁₂, β₂₁) represent cross-species transmission. This framework generalizes to any number of host species.

Basic Reproduction Number for Multi-host Systems

Dobson (2004, American Naturalist) derived R₀ for multi-host systems. For a two-host system:

Multi-host R₀
R₀ = ½[(R₀₁ + R₀₂) + √((R₀₁ + R₀₂)² - 4(R₀₁R₀₂ - R₀₁₂R₀₂₁))]
Where:
R₀₁ = β₁₁S₁/δ₁ (within-species R₀ for host 1)
R₀₂ = β₂₂S₂/δ₂ (within-species R₀ for host 2)
R₀₁₂ = β₁₂S₂/δ₁ (cross-species contribution)
R₀₂₁ = β₂₁S₁/δ₂ (cross-species contribution)
Key Insight: The multi-host R₀ exceeds the maximum of individual host R₀ values when cross-species transmission occurs. Even if R₀₁ < 1 (pathogen cannot persist in species 1 alone), the pathogen may persist in the system if R₀₂ > 1 and cross-species transmission maintains infection in species 1. This is the mathematical basis for reservoir hosts and spillover dynamics.

Consequences of Multi-host Parasite Systems

1. Apparent Competition and Extirpation

We've already explored apparent competition with squirrels and moths. The key prediction from models (Rushton et al. 2000) is that apparent competition leads to extirpation when:

  • Encounter rates between host species are moderate to high
  • Cross-species transmission rates (β₁₂, β₂₁) are substantial
  • One host species has significantly lower tolerance (higher δ)

2. Spillover: Reservoir Hosts and Dead-end Hosts

Defining Spillover Events

Spillover occurs when a pathogen is transmitted from one host population (typically its primary or reservoir host) to another host population (typically a secondary or dead-end host). Fenton and Pedersen (2005) proposed a framework classifying multi-host systems:

True Multi-host Pathogen:

  • High exposure/susceptibility in multiple host species (criterion ii)
  • Persistent transmission within all host species (criterion a)
  • Example: Influenza A virus in waterfowl, swine, and humans

Spillover from Reservoir:

  • High exposure/susceptibility in secondary host (criterion ii)
  • Non-persistent in secondary host—cannot sustain transmission alone (criterion b)
  • Example: Rabies virus from bats/raccoons to humans

Dead-end Spillover:

  • Low exposure/susceptibility in secondary host (criterion i)
  • Non-persistent (criterion b)
  • Example: West Nile virus in humans (mosquito-bird cycle with incidental human infection)
Take-Home Message: Understanding whether a pathogen is a true multi-host generalist versus a specialist with occasional spillover has profound implications for control. True multi-host pathogens require coordinated intervention across all competent host species, whereas controlling reservoir hosts alone may eliminate spillover events.

3. Dilution Effects: When Biodiversity Reduces Disease

Counter to the intuition that more host species increase disease risk, the dilution effect hypothesis proposes that biodiversity can reduce disease in focal hosts. This occurs when:

  • Poor amplifiers present: Additional host species are less competent—they become infected but transmit poorly
  • Wasted transmission: Vectors or free-living stages contact non-competent hosts, reducing transmission to competent hosts
  • Encounter reduction: Higher species diversity reduces encounter rates between competent hosts
Visual Memory Anchor: Picture a disease as a ball being thrown in a crowd. In a crowd of all basketball players (competent hosts), every throw reaches someone tall who catches it easily and throws it far. Now add many children (poor hosts) to the crowd. The ball still gets thrown, but often lands with someone who can barely catch it and throws weakly. The overall transmission network weakens despite more potential hosts.

The most famous dilution effect example is Lyme disease in North America. White-footed mice are highly competent reservoirs, while opossums, squirrels, and many bird species are poor reservoirs. Forest fragments with low diversity are dominated by mice and show high nymphal tick infection prevalence, while diverse forests show lower prevalence.

Key Insight: The dilution effect remains controversial because it depends on specific community composition. Adding more host species can increase, decrease, or have no effect on disease depending on the competence of added species. Generalization requires knowing the host competence hierarchy.

Major Take-Home Messages

  1. Multi-host pathogens are the norm: Contrary to traditional single-host models, 60-80 percent of pathogens infect multiple species, necessitating community-level approaches.
  2. Apparent competition is a powerful ecological force: Shared parasites can drive host species to extinction even without resource competition, with the outcome determined by differential tolerance (P* rule).
  3. Vector-borne and environmental transmission favor generalism: Transmission modes that bypass intimate contact enable broader host ranges.
  4. Phylogenetic relatedness constrains but doesn't determine host range: Closely related hosts share more parasites, but long-range jumps occur, especially for rapidly evolving viruses.
  5. Mathematical coupling through cross-species transmission: Multi-host models show that epidemiological dynamics become interdependent, creating emergent system behaviors not predictable from single-species models.
  6. System R₀ exceeds component R₀ values: Cross-species transmission can maintain pathogens in hosts where they couldn't persist alone, explaining reservoir dynamics and spillover.
  7. Biodiversity effects on disease are context-dependent: More species can increase disease (amplification) or decrease disease (dilution) depending on the competence of added species.
  8. Control requires multi-species strategies: Interventions must consider all competent hosts in the transmission network, not just focal species.
Final Insight: Community epidemiology reveals that understanding infectious disease requires understanding ecology. Pathogen dynamics emerge from complex species interactions, food webs, competition, predation, and biodiversity. The boundary between disease ecology and community ecology dissolves—they are inseparable perspectives on the same biological reality.
