What Causes Infectious Disease?

Before diving into the diversity of parasites, we must first understand what causes infectious disease. The answer lies in the complex interactions between hosts and various infectious agents that we collectively call parasites or pathogens.

Parasite: Everyday Definition

Parasites are infectious agents that multiply in, on, or outside hosts, can be transferred from one host to another, and cause infectious disease.

This everyday definition encompasses:

  • Usually organisms: bacteria, worms, viruses, fungi, protozoa
  • Can also be: protein molecules (prions) or cell lines (transmissible tumors like Tasmanian devil facial tumor disease)
  • Key characteristics: multiplication, transferability, disease causation

Parasite: Ecological Definition

1) Any organism that lives in or on and draws nutrients from another living organism (the host)

2) AND that reduces host fitness (is virulent) through:
• Reduced host reproduction and/or
• Elevated host mortality; reduced lifespan

Key Insight: Similarity to Predator-Prey Relationships

The ecological definition reveals that host-parasite relationships are fundamentally similar to predator-prey relationships! Both involve one organism benefiting at the expense of another, though parasites typically don't immediately kill their hosts like predators do their prey.

What is Disease?

Disease is defined as any semi-permanent condition that has a negative effect on an individual's well-being, causing discomfort, incapacity, or illness. In other words, the absence of health.

Categories of disease include:

  • Infectious disease: Caused by infectious organisms
  • Deficiency disease: Caused by nutritional deficiencies
  • Hereditary disease: Caused by genetic factors
  • Physiological disease: Caused by organ dysfunction

Infectious Disease: Results from Host-Parasite Interaction

Infectious disease manifests differently at individual and population levels:

At the individual level:

  • Fever response
  • Inflammation
  • Nausea
  • Sickness behaviors (reduced food intake, lethargy)
  • Immunopathology: cell or tissue injury caused by our own immune systems

At the population level:

  • Reduction in host fitness
  • Changes in population dynamics
  • Altered community structure

Parasite vs. Pathogen vs. Disease

It's important to distinguish between these related terms:

  • Parasite: Infectious agent (typically multicellular) that causes disease
  • Pathogen: Infectious agent (typically microbe) that causes disease
  • Infectious disease: Illness caused by infectious organism

Example: SARS-CoV-2 and COVID-19

SARS-CoV-2 is the parasite/pathogen (the infectious agent), while COVID-19 is the disease (the illness it causes). Note: Definitions for parasite vs. pathogen differ among fields; in this course, we'll mostly use them interchangeably.

The Ubiquity and Diversity of Parasites

Parasites are Everywhere

Parasitism is one of the most successful life strategies on Earth. Consider these remarkable facts:

  • Humans alone have over 1,200 species of parasites!
  • Most groups of organisms (bacteria, fungi, plants, animals) have members that are parasites
  • Most organisms on this planet can be parasitized... even bacteria!
  • An estimated 10³¹ bacteriophages exist on the planet

"Parasites are like the stars"

The vast majority of parasites have yet to be discovered. Research shows that better-studied mammals have more known parasites and infectious diseases, suggesting massive undiscovered diversity. This relationship holds across different animal groups, from primates to hoofed mammals.

Discovery Bias in Parasite Research

Studies reveal a strong correlation between research effort and the number of known parasite species:

  • Primates: More citations in literature = more known parasite species
  • Hoofed mammals: More animals sampled = more known parasite species
  • This suggests that parasite diversity is vastly underestimated across most host groups

Examples of Parasitic Diversity

Parasites span an incredible range of organisms:

Microscopic Parasites

  • Viruses (SARS-CoV-2, influenza)
  • Bacteria (tuberculosis, plague)
  • Protozoa (malaria, sleeping sickness)
  • Fungi (athlete's foot, plant rusts)

Macroscopic Parasites

  • Helminths (tapeworms, flukes, roundworms)
  • Arthropods (ticks, fleas, lice)
  • Parasitic plants (mistletoe, Indian pipe)
  • Unusual cases (sea lamprey, parasitic barnacles)

Hyperparasites

  • Parasites of parasites
  • Viruses that infect bacteria
  • Fungi that parasitize other fungi
  • "Great fleas have little fleas upon their backs to bite 'em"

Ecological Examples

  • Plant diseases (corn smut, potato blight)
  • Wildlife diseases (white-nose syndrome in bats)
  • Marine parasites (affecting ocean carbon cycles)
  • Zombie parasites (Cordyceps fungi)

Parasite Life Cycles

Understanding parasite life cycles is crucial for comprehending their population biology and developing control strategies.

Simple (Direct) Life Cycles

Parasite can complete development and reproduction with a single host species
(Parasite may have environmental stage)

Characteristics of simple life cycles:

  • Only one host species required for completion
  • May include free-living stages in the environment
  • Examples: many bacterial infections, some viral diseases, direct-transmitted helminths
  • Generally easier to control since fewer hosts are involved

Complex (Indirect) Life Cycles

Parasite requires 2 or more host species to complete development and reproduction

Definitive host: Required for sexual reproduction of parasites
Intermediate host: Required for development but not sexual reproduction

Characteristics of complex life cycles:

  • Multiple host species essential for parasite survival
  • Often involves specific developmental stages in each host
  • Examples: malaria (human and mosquito), many tapeworms (multiple vertebrate hosts)
  • More vulnerable to control efforts targeting any host in the cycle

Evolutionary Implications

Complex life cycles represent sophisticated evolutionary adaptations that allow parasites to exploit different ecological niches and transmission opportunities. However, they also create dependencies that can be exploited for disease control.

Key Differences in Life Cycle Strategies

Parasites differ in several fundamental ways related to their life cycles:

  • How they reproduce: Sexual vs. asexual, frequency, location
  • Number of hosts required: One vs. multiple host species
  • Replication location: Inside host (microparasites) vs. outside host (macroparasites)
  • Transmission mode: Direct contact, environmental stages, vectors
  • Trophic strategy: Whether host death is required for transmission

Microparasites vs. Macroparasites

One of the most fundamental distinctions in parasite biology is between microparasites and macroparasites. This classification is based on size, but more importantly on biological characteristics that affect population dynamics.

Microparasites

Small parasites including viruses, bacteria, protozoa, and fungi.

Key characteristics:

  • Size: Small (unicellular or viral)
  • Reproduction: Rapid multiplication inside host, short generation time
  • Infection outcomes: Often leads to lasting immunity or death
  • Infection duration: Transient infections with acute fitness effects
  • Transmission: Through contact, environment, or vectors
  • Pathology: Intensity-independent (symptoms don't depend on initial number of parasites)

Macroparasites

Larger parasites including helminths (worms) and arthropods.

Key characteristics:

  • Size: Large (multicellular)
  • Reproduction: Adults release infective stages outside host, longer generation times
  • Infection outcomes: Lasting immunity uncommon
  • Infection duration: Persistent and chronic infections
  • Transmission: Cannot directly lead to new parasites in same host
  • Pathology: Intensity-dependent (fitness impact relates to number of parasites)
Trait Microparasites Macroparasites
Size Small (unicellular) Large (multicellular)
Generation time Short (hours / days) Long (days / months)
Life cycle Often simple (not always) Often complex (not always)
Reproduction Inside host, asexual, rapid Outside primary host
Parasite load Extremely high Much lower
Duration of infection Short Long (life-long)
Host Immunity Strong, often life-long Weak, transient

Why This Distinction Matters

All of these traits fundamentally impact how we conceptualize and model disease dynamics mathematically! The differences between micro- and macroparasites require different mathematical approaches and have different implications for disease control strategies.

Measurement Approaches

The micro-macro distinction also affects how we study parasite infections:

For microparasites:

  • Prevalence: Proportion of population that is infected
  • Seroprevalence: Proportion of population that is seropositive (has antibodies to a given parasite)
  • Focus on presence/absence rather than intensity

For macroparasites:

  • Numbers of parasites: Actual counts of individual parasites
  • Degree of aggregation: How parasites are distributed across host populations
  • Focus on intensity and distribution patterns

Major Taxonomic Groups of Parasites

Parasites span virtually all taxonomic groups, from the simplest viruses to complex multicellular organisms. Understanding the major groups helps us appreciate the diversity of parasitic strategies and their ecological roles.

Viruses

The simplest and most abundant parasites on Earth.

Characteristics:

  • Structure: Nucleic acid core (DNA or RNA) and protein capsid
  • Reproduction: Obligate intracellular parasites that hijack host cell machinery
  • Activity: Inert outside hosts
  • Size: Extremely small (nanometer scale)

Major groups:

  • DNA viruses: Poxviridae (smallpox, monkeypox), Herpesviridae (herpes, chicken pox) - larger, more genes, slower mutation rates
  • RNA viruses: Flaviridae (dengue, yellow fever), Orthomyxoviridae (influenza), Coronaviridae (SARS-CoV-2) - smaller genomes, faster mutation rates

Ecological Importance of Viruses

Viruses are responsible for ~1/4 of carbon flux in oceans, terminate harmful algal blooms, and cause major wildlife epidemics. Bacteriophages alone outnumber all other life forms and play crucial roles in ecosystem functioning.

Bacteria

Unicellular prokaryotes with diverse parasitic strategies.

Characteristics:

  • Structure: No mitochondria, unbound genetic material
  • Location: Can be intracellular or extracellular parasites
  • Reproduction: Asexual reproduction, rapid generation times
  • Genetics: Plasmids can be transferred between cells (horizontal gene transfer)
  • Resistance: Some produce resistant spores (e.g., Bacillus anthracis)

Important bacterial diseases:

  • Historical: Black death (Yersinia pestis), tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis)
  • Contemporary: Cholera (Vibrio cholerae), bacterial STDs (Chlamydia, gonorrhea)
  • Opportunistic: Many bacteria are normally commensal but can cause disease under certain conditions

Fungi

Eukaryotic organisms with rigid cell walls that often behave as microparasites.

Characteristics:

  • Structure: Rigid cell wall made of chitin
  • Growth: Vegetative growth via fungal mycelium or yeast-like budding
  • Infection: Elongated hyphae can grow through host cells
  • Reproduction: Produce sexual or asexual spores
  • Ecology: Many live as free-living saprophytes feeding on dead material

Major disease examples:

  • Plant diseases: Potato blight (Irish famine), powdery mildews, rusts, smuts
  • Human diseases: Ringworm, athlete's foot (Trichophyton spp.)
  • Wildlife diseases: White-nose syndrome in bats (Geomyces destructans), frog chytrid (Batrachochytrium)
  • Invertebrate diseases: Cordyceps "zombie" fungi, Metschnikowia in Daphnia

Protozoa

Single-celled eukaryotes with extreme diversity in form and function.

Characteristics:

  • Diversity: Extremely diverse - many phyla with wide range of shapes
  • Transmission: Varied transmission strategies and tissues affected
  • Location: Can be intra- or extracellular parasites
  • Host range: Can be host specific or broad in range
  • Behavior: Typically "act" like microparasites

Vector-borne examples:

  • Plasmodium: Malaria (transmitted by mosquitoes)
  • Trypanosoma: Sleeping sickness (transmitted by tsetse flies)
  • Leishmania: Leishmaniasis (transmitted by sand flies)

Helminths (Parasitic Worms)

Multicellular animals that behave as macroparasites with complex life cycles.

Nematodes (Roundworms)

  • Diversity: Most diverse group of helminths
  • Transmission: Skin penetration, egg ingestion, vectors, intermediate hosts
  • Attachment: Most lack holdfast mechanisms (except hookworms)
  • Examples: Hookworm, Guinea worm, heartworm in dogs

Cestodes (Tapeworms)

  • Structure: Parasitic flatworms, lack digestive system
  • Nutrition: Absorb nutrients through body surface
  • Attachment: Head-like scolex with suction cups
  • Life cycle: Often complex, requiring intermediate hosts

Trematodes (Flukes)

  • Monogenean trematodes: One sucker, simple life cycles
  • Digenean trematodes: Two suckers, complex life cycles with multiple hosts
  • Location: Adults parasitize liver, intestinal tract
  • Stages: Many life stages (eggs, miracidia, rediae, cercariae, metacercariae, adults)

Acanthocephalans (Thorny-headed worms)

  • Attachment: Forcibly evert spiny proboscis into gut wall
  • Life cycle: Complex cycles involving invertebrates, fish, amphibians, birds, mammals
  • Diversity: Approximately 1,400 species

Arthropods

Some arthropods are parasites themselves, while others serve as vectors or intermediate hosts.

Parasitic arthropods:

  • Chelicerata: Ticks, mites
  • Insecta: Bot flies, lice, fleas
  • Adaptations: Many are ectoparasites with special holdfast mechanisms

Vector arthropods:

  • Mosquitoes: Transmit viruses, protozoa, filarial worms
  • Flies: Transmit bacteria, protozoa
  • Ticks and fleas: Transmit bacteria, viruses

Classification Challenge

Should we classify vector arthropods as parasites? They do feed on host blood and can cause harm, but their primary role is often as a vehicle for other parasites. This highlights the complexity of parasitic relationships and the sometimes blurry boundaries in classification systems.

Take-Home Messages

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Parasites are ubiquitous and diverse: Found in virtually all taxonomic groups and ecological niches
  • Infectious disease arises from host-parasite interactions: Not just the parasite, but the relationship that matters
  • Extreme biological diversity: From viruses to large arthropods, with vastly different life strategies
  • Micro- vs. macroparasites: Fundamental differences in population biology and modeling approaches
  • Life cycle complexity: Simple vs. complex cycles have major implications for transmission and control
  • Taxonomic breadth: Major groups include viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoans, helminths, and arthropods
  • Ecological importance: Parasites play crucial roles in ecosystem functioning and population regulation

Looking Forward

These fundamental concepts about parasite diversity and biology form the foundation for understanding more complex topics in population biology of infectious diseases, including:

  • Mathematical modeling of disease dynamics
  • Transmission mechanisms and modes
  • Host-parasite coevolution
  • Disease control strategies
  • Ecological and evolutionary impacts of parasitism
