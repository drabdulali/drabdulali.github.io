Coevolution: The Fundamental Concept

Coevolution is evolution by natural selection in populations of two closely interacting species, in which evolutionary changes in each species directly shapes the fitness of the other.

Types of Coevolution

Mutualistic Coevolution

All participants benefit with increased fitness as a result of the interaction. Examples include pollination mutualisms and resource consumption relationships.

Classic Example: Darwin's star orchid (Angraecum sesquipedale) and its hawkmoth pollinator evolved together. The orchid has an extremely long nectar spur (up to 30 cm), and Darwin predicted the existence of a moth with an equally long proboscis before it was discovered. This demonstrates how selection pressures from one species drive adaptive changes in another, with both species benefiting from the relationship.

Antagonistic Coevolution

One participant suffers as a result of the interaction. This includes predation, herbivory, and parasitism. In host-parasite systems, this leads to evolutionary "arms races" where:

  • Parasites evolve to infect hosts more effectively (increased virulence or infectivity)
  • Hosts evolve to resist, tolerate, or avoid infection
  • Costs may limit both evolutionary responses, preventing indefinite escalation
Key Insight: In host-parasite antagonistic coevolution, neither side achieves permanent victory. Instead, the interaction creates a dynamic equilibrium where both hosts and parasites continually adapt to each other, maintaining genetic diversity in both populations.

Gene-for-Gene Model of Coevolution

The gene-for-gene model, originally described by H.H. Flor in 1956 studying flax and flax rust, proposes a specific genetic architecture underlying host-parasite interactions.

Model Assumptions

  • Resistance gene is dominant in host: Hosts carrying at least one copy of the resistance allele (R) can resist infection
  • Infectivity (virulence) gene is recessive in pathogen: Parasites need two copies of the virulence allele (a) to overcome host resistance
  • Multiple alleles exist: Both host and pathogen populations harbor many such alleles
  • Matching specificity: For each resistance gene, there is usually a corresponding gene in the pathogen that can overcome that resistance
  • Costs constrain evolution: Both resistance and infectivity impose fitness costs

Case Study: Flax and Flax Rust (Linum marginale and Melampsora lini)

System Description: Flax rust is a fungal pathogen that causes significant disease in flax plants. The rust has a complex life cycle alternating between woody and herbaceous plant hosts, with wind-dispersed spores that can rapidly spread through flax populations.

Findings: Flor's pioneering work identified specific resistance genes in flax that corresponded to avirulence genes in the rust fungus. This one-to-one correspondence became the foundation of the gene-for-gene hypothesis.

Take-Home Message: The gene-for-gene model provides a mechanistic explanation for how hosts and parasites can maintain genetic diversity through balancing selection, with each genotype's success depending on the frequency of matching genotypes in the opposing population.

Infection Matrix: Gene-for-Gene Interactions

Pathogen / Host RR (Resistant) Rr (Resistant) rr (Susceptible)
AA (Avirulent) No Infection No Infection Infection
Aa (Avirulent) No Infection No Infection Infection
aa (Virulent) Infection Infection Infection
Key Pattern: The aa (virulent) parasite genotype can infect all host types, but this universally infective genotype pays a cost. In the absence of resistant hosts, avirulent parasites (A_) have higher fitness. This creates frequency-dependent selection where the most common host genotype selects for the matching virulent parasite, which then selects for new resistant host genotypes.

Costs of Resistance and Virulence

Both resistance in hosts and virulence in parasites come with fitness costs:

  • Cost of resistance: Hosts carrying resistance genes may have reduced growth rates, reproduction, or competitive ability when parasites are absent. This explains why susceptible genotypes persist in populations.
  • Cost of virulence: Parasites capable of overcoming more resistance genes show reduced transmission rates or spore production. Research by Thrall and Burdon (2003) demonstrated that parasites with broader host ranges had lower fecundity.

Fitness Trade-off Relationships

Host fitness with resistance:

Whost = Wmax - cresistance

Parasite fitness with virulence:

Wparasite = βmax - cvirulence × n

Where:

  • W = fitness
  • c = cost parameter
  • β = transmission rate
  • n = number of resistance genes overcome

Case Study: Australian Flax System (Thrall & Burdon 2003)

Experimental Design: Researchers surveyed natural populations of Linum marginale and its rust pathogen Melampsora lini across multiple sites, measuring host resistance and pathogen virulence frequencies.

Key Results:

  • Strong positive correlation between mean host resistance and mean pathogen virulence across populations
  • Pathogens were locally adapted but showed decreased fecundity with increasing virulence breadth
  • The relationship showed diminishing returns, consistent with costs of resistance

Take-Home Message: Empirical data supports the gene-for-gene model predictions, showing that virulence evolves in response to host resistance but is constrained by fitness costs, preventing evolution of a universally infective "super-parasite."

Matching Alleles Model of Coevolution

The matching alleles model represents an alternative framework for understanding host-parasite coevolution. Unlike gene-for-gene, this model proposes that parasites must precisely match host genotypes to successfully infect.

Model Characteristics

  • Lock-and-key mechanism: Parasites can only infect hosts with matching genotypes
  • No universal infectivity: No single parasite genotype can infect all hosts
  • No universal resistance: No single host genotype resists all parasites
  • Symmetrical interaction: The interaction is more balanced than in gene-for-gene

Infection Matrix: Matching Alleles

Parasite / Host AB Ab aB ab
AB Infection No Infection No Infection No Infection
Ab No Infection Infection No Infection No Infection
aB No Infection No Infection Infection No Infection
ab No Infection No Infection No Infection Infection
Critical Difference from Gene-for-Gene: In the matching alleles model, infection only occurs when host and parasite genotypes precisely match (diagonal elements). This creates strong negative frequency-dependent selection, where rare genotypes have a selective advantage simply because parasites are not adapted to them.

Negative Frequency-Dependent Selection

The matching alleles model generates negative frequency-dependent selection, where the fitness of a genotype decreases as it becomes more common. This occurs because:

  1. Common host genotypes are targeted by parasites, which evolve to match them
  2. Rare host genotypes escape parasite pressure because few parasites are adapted to infect them
  3. As rare genotypes increase in frequency, parasites adapt to match them
  4. The formerly common genotypes (now rare) gain an advantage
  5. This creates oscillating dynamics in genotype frequencies

Frequency-Dependent Selection

Change in host allele frequency:

Δphost = phost(1 - phost)[s(1 - 2pparasite)]

Change in parasite allele frequency:

Δpparasite = pparasite(1 - pparasite)[s(2phost - 1)]

Where:

  • p = allele frequency
  • s = selection coefficient

Note: Parasites "track" hosts with a time lag, creating oscillations

Case Study: Daphnia magna and Pasteuria ramosa (Carius et al. 2001)

System Description: Daphnia magna (water flea) is infected by Pasteuria ramosa, a bacterial parasite that castrates its host. Daphnia reproduce by cyclical parthenogenesis (alternating between asexual and sexual reproduction), allowing maintenance of distinct clonal lineages.

Experimental Approach: Researchers created infection matrices by exposing multiple Daphnia clones to multiple Pasteuria isolates in all pairwise combinations.

Key Results:

  • Highly specific infection patterns with no universally infective parasite or universally resistant host
  • Strong evidence for matching alleles dynamics (diagonal patterns in infection matrices)
  • Local adaptation: parasites were more infective on sympatric (local) hosts than allopatric (foreign) hosts

Take-Home Message: The Daphnia-Pasteuria system provides strong empirical support for matching alleles dynamics and demonstrates that specific genetic matching, rather than simple virulence-resistance, can drive host-parasite coevolution.

Red Queen Dynamics

The Red Queen hypothesis, named after Lewis Carroll's character in "Through the Looking Glass" who said "it takes all the running you can do to keep in the same place," proposes that organisms must constantly adapt just to maintain their relative fitness.

The Red Queen Hypothesis and Sexual Reproduction

One of the most compelling applications of the Red Queen hypothesis is as an explanation for the evolution and maintenance of sexual reproduction. This addresses a fundamental puzzle in evolutionary biology:

The Cost of Sex

The Paradox: Sexual reproduction has substantial costs compared to asexual reproduction:

  • Only 50% of offspring carry your genes (compared to 100% in asexual reproduction)
  • Time and energy spent finding mates
  • Risk of sexually transmitted diseases
  • Breaking up favorable gene combinations through recombination

The Solution: Parasites may provide the selective pressure that maintains sex. Through negative frequency-dependent selection, sexual reproduction generates rare genotype combinations that escape parasite adaptation.

Key Predictions of Red Queen Dynamics

If parasites track host genotypes through negative frequency-dependent selection, we expect:

  1. Parasites should be most infective on common hosts and less able to infect rare hosts
  2. Parasites should better infect contemporary hosts than those common in the past or future (temporal tracking)
  3. Parasites should better infect local (sympatric) hosts than those from elsewhere (spatial adaptation)

Red Queen Oscillations

Host-parasite frequencies oscillate over time:

phost(t) = A sin(ωt + φ1)

pparasite(t) = A sin(ωt + φ2)

Where:

  • A = amplitude of oscillation
  • ω = angular frequency (2π/period)
  • φ = phase angle (φ2 - φ1 represents time lag)
  • Parasite oscillations lag behind host oscillations

Over long timescales, frequencies cycle but average values remain relatively constant ("running to stay in place").

Case Study: Resurrection Ecology with Daphnia (Decaestecker et al. 2007, 2013)

Revolutionary Approach: Researchers extracted dormant Daphnia eggs and Pasteuria spores from dated sediment layers in lake cores, effectively "resurrecting" hosts and parasites from the past.

Experimental Design:

  • Sediment cores representing 100+ years of evolutionary history
  • Cross-temporal infection experiments: past parasites × current hosts, current parasites × past hosts, etc.
  • Each sediment layer represented 10-20 host generations

Key Results:

  • Temporal tracking: Parasites from any given time period were most infective on hosts from the same time period
  • Oscillating dynamics: Infection success showed cyclical patterns consistent with Red Queen predictions
  • Amplitude changes: Oscillations had larger amplitudes in the past (fewer resistance genes) and smaller amplitudes more recently (more resistance genes accumulated)
  • Asymmetric tracking: Hosts from the past were more vulnerable to future parasites (large amplitude) than recent hosts were to past parasites (small amplitude)

Take-Home Message: The sediment core studies provide the most direct evidence for Red Queen dynamics in nature, demonstrating that parasites continuously track and adapt to host populations over evolutionary time. This supports the hypothesis that parasite-mediated selection maintains genetic diversity and favors sexual reproduction.

Major Implication: Parasites may be responsible for the evolution and maintenance of sexual reproduction in most eukaryotes. Without the constantly changing selective pressure from rapidly evolving parasites, asexual reproduction would likely outcompete sexual reproduction in most circumstances.

Local Adaptation

Case Study: Geographic Variation in Daphnia-Pasteuria (Ebert 1994)

Experimental Design: Pasteuria parasites collected from different lakes were tested for their ability to infect Daphnia hosts from the same lake (sympatric) versus different lakes (allopatric).

Results: Parasites showed higher growth rates and infectivity when paired with sympatric hosts compared to novel (allopatric) hosts, demonstrating local adaptation.

Take-Home Message: Parasites are locally adapted to their host populations, supporting the prediction that parasite evolution tracks local host genotype frequencies through space as well as time.

Synthesis: Comparing Models and Evidence

Gene-for-Gene vs. Matching Alleles

Feature Gene-for-Gene Matching Alleles
Interaction Type Asymmetric (parasites can overcome) Symmetric (must match exactly)
Universal Infectivity Possible (virulent genotype) Impossible
Selection Pattern Directional (arms race) Balancing (cycling)
Diversity Maintenance Through costs and escalation Through negative frequency-dependence
Empirical Support Plant-pathogen systems Daphnia-Pasteuria system
Important Note: These models are not mutually exclusive. Natural systems may exhibit characteristics of both models, with some genes following gene-for-gene dynamics and others following matching alleles patterns. The models represent idealized endpoints of a continuum of host-parasite genetic interactions.

Broad Conclusions

  • Hosts and parasites coevolve together in dynamic, reciprocal evolutionary processes
  • Both gene-for-gene and matching alleles models promote genetic diversity in both hosts and parasites
  • Negative frequency-dependent selection provides the best explanation for the evolution and maintenance of sexual reproduction (Red Queen Hypothesis)
  • Parasites may have been the primary selective force driving the evolution of sex in eukaryotes
  • Costs constrain both resistance evolution in hosts and virulence evolution in parasites
  • Empirical evidence from multiple systems supports coevolutionary theory predictions
