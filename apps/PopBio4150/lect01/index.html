What is Disease Ecology?

Ecology is the study of interactions between organisms and their environment. Disease ecology extends this concept to focus specifically on the interactions between pathogens and their environment.

For parasites and pathogens, the "environment" is primarily the host, but also includes:

  • The host population
  • The broader community of species
  • External abiotic factors (temperature, humidity, etc.)
Key Insight: Disease ecology asks: How do parasites and pathogens spread through, influence, and evolve within host populations, communities, and ecosystems?

Why Study Disease Ecology?

  1. Prevalence of parasitism: Most organisms in nature are parasitic
  2. Health consequences: Critical for human health, wildlife health, biodiversity, and conservation
  3. Scientific fascination: Parasites exhibit remarkable adaptations and life cycles

Case Study 1: Hendravirus in Australian Flying Foxes

Hendravirus represents a classic example of an emerging zoonotic disease - one that can spread from animals to humans.

Pathogen Characteristics

  • Type: RNA virus (Paramyxoviridae family)
  • Transmission: Shed in bat urine with 1-week infectious period
  • Host effects: Asymptomatic in bats, highly lethal (50-100% mortality) in horses and humans
  • Symptoms in spillover hosts: Respiratory illness, pulmonary edema, encephalitis

Ecological Drivers of Emergence

Habitat Fragmentation and Urbanization: Deforestation has forced flying foxes to establish permanent camps near urban areas where fruit trees provide reliable food sources. This increases contact between bats and domestic animals/humans.

Climate and Food Stress: El Niño events and droughts reduce natural food availability, forcing bats into closer contact with human settlements and increasing viral shedding due to nutritional stress.

Spillover Risk ∝ (Bat Density × Stress Level × Human-Animal Contact Rate)

Population Dynamics and Herd Immunity

Research by Plowright et al. (2011) revealed that larger, more connected bat populations maintain higher levels of herd immunity, reducing viral shedding. Fragmented populations have lower immunity levels, leading to increased spillover risk.

Key Insight: Habitat preservation and maintaining large, connected wildlife populations can reduce zoonotic disease emergence by supporting natural herd immunity.

Case Study 2: Avian Malaria in Endemic Hawaiian Birds

This case demonstrates how introduced pathogens can devastate naive host populations and how climate change can exacerbate disease impacts.

The Hawaiian Honeycreeper Crisis

Hawaiian honeycreepers underwent an adaptive radiation producing ~40 species. However, 50% are now extinct, and 50% of remaining species are threatened or endangered.

Disease Introduction Timeline

  • Pre-1830: No mosquitoes present in Hawaii
  • 1830s onward: Introduction of mosquito vectors (especially Culex quinquefasciatus)
  • Early 1900s: Arrival of avian malaria (Plasmodium relictum)
  • 1900s-present: Massive population declines and range shifts in native birds

Pathogen Effects

On native birds: 65-90% mortality rate, with symptoms including anemia, hepatitis, splenitis, and encephalitis.

On introduced birds: Act as competent reservoirs with minimal disease effects, maintaining the parasite in the ecosystem.

Elevational Refugia

Native birds have retreated to high elevations (>1,200m) where cooler temperatures limit mosquito development and malaria transmission. This creates a "thermal refuge" but also constrains available habitat.

Transmission Rate ∝ Temperature × Mosquito Density × Bird Density

Climate Change Impacts

A 2°C temperature increase could eliminate most malaria-free habitat, pushing the thermal refuge higher up mountains and potentially causing additional extinctions.

Evolutionary Response

Some Hawaiian Amakihi populations have evolved tolerance to malaria in <100 years, demonstrating rapid evolutionary adaptation under strong selection pressure.

Key Insight: Climate change can turn previously safe refugia into disease hotspots, requiring adaptive management strategies that account for shifting environmental conditions.

Case Study 3: Measles in Human Populations

Measles provides a classic example of epidemic dynamics and the importance of critical community size for pathogen persistence.

Pathogen Characteristics

  • Type: Highly contagious RNA virus (Morbillivirus)
  • Transmission: Airborne droplets, R₀ ≈ 12-18
  • Incubation: 4-12 days
  • Immunity: Lifelong after infection or vaccination

Critical Community Size

Measles requires a minimum population size of approximately 300,000-500,000 people to persist endemically. Below this threshold, the virus goes extinct between epidemics due to insufficient susceptible hosts.

Critical Community Size = (Birth Rate × R₀) / (Recovery Rate × Contact Rate)

Epidemic Patterns

Seasonal Forcing: School calendar creates annual pulses of high transmission, leading to predictable epidemic cycles.

Biennial Cycles: In large populations, measles often exhibits 2-year epidemic cycles due to the interplay between seasonal transmission and susceptible recruitment.

Vaccination and Control

The measles vaccine (introduced 1963) dramatically reduced case numbers. However, vaccine hesitancy can lead to local outbreaks when coverage drops below the herd immunity threshold (~95%).

Key Insight: Population size determines whether pathogens persist or go extinct locally. Understanding critical thresholds is essential for disease control and elimination strategies.

The "BIG" Picture: Key Principles of Disease Ecology

  • Host density and population size are crucial for pathogen persistence and epidemic dynamics
  • Social encounters and contact rates drive transmission and can be influenced by seasonality, behavior, and environmental factors
  • Disease-induced extinction and endangerment represent major conservation threats, especially for isolated or small populations
  • Reservoir hosts and introduced species can maintain pathogens in ecosystems and facilitate spillover to naive hosts
  • Climate effects on host-parasite interactions can shift disease ranges and create new epidemiological patterns
  • Shared infections between humans and animals (zoonoses) represent emerging disease threats requiring One Health approaches
  • Anthropogenic change can drive disease emergence through habitat modification, species introductions, and climate change
  • Mathematical models provide essential tools for understanding disease dynamics and evaluating control strategies
