Fundamental Definitions

Resistance

Resistance refers to the ability of a host to prevent or limit parasite infection. It is the inverse of susceptibility. A resistant host is rarely or never infected, while a susceptible host is mostly or always infected when exposed to a parasite.

Key Insight: Resistance is a quantitative trait that exists on a continuum. Hosts are not simply "resistant" or "susceptible" but rather exhibit varying degrees of resistance depending on their genotype, physiological state, and environmental conditions.

Susceptibility

Susceptibility is the opposite of resistance. It measures how likely a host is to become infected when exposed to a parasite. High susceptibility means the host is easily infected, while low susceptibility indicates the host can effectively prevent infection.

Tolerance

Tolerance refers to the ability of a host to maintain fitness (survival, growth, reproduction) despite being infected. Unlike resistance, tolerance does not reduce parasite load but instead minimizes the damage caused by infection.

Mathematical Representation of Tolerance:

W = W0 - m × P

Where:

W = host fitness when infected

W0 = host fitness when uninfected

m = slope of fitness decline (tolerance = 1/m)

P = parasite load

A more tolerant host has a smaller slope (m), meaning fitness declines less rapidly with increasing parasite load.

Mechanisms of Host Resistance

1. Immune Defenses

The immune system provides the primary defense against parasites through both innate and adaptive mechanisms:

Innate Immunity: The first line of defense, including physical barriers (skin, mucous membranes), chemical barriers (antimicrobial peptides, pH), and cellular responses (phagocytes, natural killer cells). Innate immunity is rapid but non-specific.

Adaptive Immunity: A learned, specific response involving B cells (antibody production) and T cells (cell-mediated immunity). Adaptive immunity provides immunological memory, allowing faster and stronger responses upon re-exposure to the same parasite.

Key Insight: The strength and specificity of immune responses vary greatly among individuals due to genetic variation in immune genes, including the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC), which plays a crucial role in pathogen recognition and presentation.

2. Physical Barriers

Physical barriers prevent parasites from entering host tissues. Examples include:

  • Skin thickness and composition
  • Mucous membrane integrity
  • Shell structures in invertebrates
  • Cuticle thickness in plants and arthropods

3. Behavioral Avoidance

Hosts can reduce exposure to parasites through behavioral modifications:

  • Avoiding contaminated water sources
  • Grooming behaviors to remove ectoparasites
  • Selecting nesting sites with lower parasite prevalence
  • Reducing social contact when infected individuals are present

4. Lack of Susceptible Tissue

Some hosts lack the specific receptors or tissues required for parasite infection. This form of resistance is often highly effective but genetically constrained.

Example: CCR5-Δ32 Mutation and HIV Resistance

Individuals homozygous for a 32-base-pair deletion in the CCR5 gene lack functional CCR5 receptors on their cell surfaces. Since most HIV strains require CCR5 to enter cells, these individuals are highly resistant to HIV infection. This represents resistance through the absence of necessary host tissue (functional receptors).

Take-home message: Genetic mutations that eliminate pathogen entry points can provide strong resistance, but such mutations may have other fitness consequences in the absence of the pathogen.

Case Study: Sickle Cell Anemia and Heterozygote Advantage

Background

Sickle cell disease is caused by a single amino acid substitution in the beta-globin gene (hemoglobin subunit). The mutation changes glutamic acid to valine at position 6, resulting in abnormal hemoglobin that causes red blood cells to adopt a sickle shape under low oxygen conditions.

Genotypes and Phenotypes

Genotype Red Blood Cell Shape Malaria Susceptibility Health Impact
AA (homozygous normal) Round, normal Susceptible No sickle cell disease
Aa (heterozygous) Mostly round, some sickling Resistant Mild sickle cell trait
aa (homozygous sickle) Sickled Resistant Severe/fatal sickle cell disease

Mechanism of Resistance

Sickled red blood cells provide resistance to malaria through multiple mechanisms:

  • Sickled cells are rapidly cleared from circulation, reducing parasite replication time
  • The abnormal cell environment inhibits Plasmodium growth and development
  • Sickled cells display surface markers that trigger earlier immune recognition
  • Reduced oxygen availability in sickled cells inhibits parasite metabolism

Heterozygote Advantage

The sickle cell allele persists at high frequencies in malaria-endemic regions despite causing severe disease in homozygotes because heterozygotes (Aa) have the highest fitness. They gain malaria resistance without suffering severe anemia.

Fitness Values in Malaria-Endemic Regions:

WAA < WAa > Waa

Example fitness values:

WAA = 0.85 (dies from malaria)

WAa = 1.0 (resistant to malaria, mild symptoms)

Waa = 0.2 (dies from sickle cell disease)

Geographic Distribution

The sickle cell allele arose independently at least four times in human populations, always in regions with high malaria prevalence. The allele frequency reaches 10-20% in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia where malaria is endemic but is rare in regions without malaria.

Take-home Message: The sickle cell case demonstrates balancing selection, where genetic variation is maintained because heterozygotes have higher fitness than either homozygote. This illustrates how parasites can drive the evolution and maintenance of genetic polymorphisms in host populations, and how resistance alleles can have severe costs that prevent them from going to fixation.

Case Study: Myxomatosis in Australian Rabbits

Historical Context

European rabbits were introduced to Australia in 1859 for hunting. By 1920, the population had exploded to an estimated 10 billion rabbits, causing massive agricultural damage and ecological disruption.

Introduction of Myxomatosis Virus (1950)

The Australian government deliberately introduced the highly virulent myxoma virus as a biological control agent in 1950. The initial population was approximately 1.8 billion rabbits.

Initial Impact

1950-1952: The virus killed 99.8% of exposed rabbits. Case fatality rate was greater than 99%. This appeared to be a spectacular success in biocontrol.

1952-1954: Case fatality rate declined to approximately 90%.

By 1958: Only 30% of exposed rabbits died from the virus.

What happened? Both the virus and the rabbits evolved rapidly.

Viral Evolution: Decreased Virulence

The virus evolved to become less virulent. Data from field isolates showed a dramatic shift in virulence grades:

Time Period Grade I (>99% fatal) Grade III (70-95% fatal) Grade V (<50% fatal)
1950-1951 100% 0% 0%
1952-1955 13.3% 53.3% 0%
1955-1958 0.7% 54.6% 15.5%

Why Did Virulence Evolve Downward?

The myxoma virus is transmitted by mosquitoes and fleas, which require live hosts to feed on. Extremely virulent strains that killed rabbits within 10-13 days provided less opportunity for transmission than moderately virulent strains that kept hosts alive for 20-30 days with external lesions.

Trade-off Between Transmission and Virulence:

R0 = β × D × S/N

Where:

β = transmission rate (opportunities for transmission)

D = duration of infectiousness (increases with lower virulence)

S/N = proportion of susceptible hosts

Optimal virulence maximizes R0 by balancing transmission rate against host survival.

Host Evolution: Increased Resistance

Simultaneously, rabbits evolved resistance to the virus. Experimental infections in the 1960s-1970s showed:

Location Year Virus Grade Case Fatality Rate
Norfolk 1966 III 90%
Norfolk 1970 III 59%
Norfolk 1974 III 13%

When challenged with the same standard virus strain (Grade III), rabbits from later years showed dramatically improved survival, indicating genetic evolution of resistance.

Form of Selection

Rabbits: Directional selection favoring increased resistance. The mean resistance in the population shifted toward higher values.

Virus: Stabilizing selection toward intermediate virulence, balancing transmission opportunity against host survival.

Take-home Message: The myxomatosis case demonstrates rapid coevolution of both host and parasite in response to strong selection pressure. It illustrates that parasites may not evolve to maximum virulence when transmission depends on host survival, and that hosts can rapidly evolve resistance when genetic variation exists. This case became a classic example of "failed" biocontrol, highlighting the importance of evolutionary thinking in disease management.

Case Study: Soay Sheep and Costs of Resistance

Background

Soay sheep are a primitive breed living on the island of St. Kilda, Scotland. This population has been intensively studied since the 1980s, providing insights into host-parasite dynamics and the evolution of resistance in a natural setting.

The Parasite System

The sheep are infected by macroparasitic nematode worms (primarily Teladorsagia circumcincta and Trichostrongylus spp.) that live in the gastrointestinal tract. Parasite infections contribute significantly to population cycles, with major die-offs occurring during harsh winters.

Autoantibodies as a Measure of Immune Responsiveness

Researchers measured antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) as a proxy for overall immune system activity. ANAs are antibodies that bind to components of the cell nucleus. While typically associated with autoimmune disorders in humans, in Soay sheep, elevated ANA levels indicate heightened antibody responsiveness in general.

Distribution of ANAs in the Population

  • Approximately 12% of sheep carry elevated ANAs (ANA-positive)
  • ANA levels increase with age
  • Females have higher ANA levels than males
  • ANA-positive status is heritable, indicating a genetic basis

Benefits of High ANAs: Survival During Harsh Winters

During population crash years (characterized by high population density, limited food, and heavy parasite loads), ANA-positive sheep had significantly higher survival rates:

Condition ANA-Negative Survival ANA-Positive Survival
Crash Years 65% 74%

This suggests that high immune responsiveness (indicated by ANA-positive status) protects against parasite-induced mortality when conditions are harsh and parasite loads are high.

Costs of High ANAs: Reduced Survival in Good Years

However, during years with low parasite pressure (non-crash years), the pattern reversed:

Condition ANA-Negative Survival ANA-Positive Survival
Non-Crash Years 95% 90%

When parasite pressure was low, ANA-positive sheep had reduced survival, demonstrating a cost to maintaining high immune responsiveness in the absence of strong parasitic challenge.

Reproductive Costs

ANA-positive individuals also experienced reproductive costs:

Males: ANA-positive males had reduced siring success. Only about 18% of ANA-positive males successfully sired offspring, compared to 50% of ANA-negative males.

Females: ANA-positive females had lower breeding rates (80% vs. 85% for ANA-negative females) and their lambs had slightly lower survival rates (92% vs. 85%).

Take-home Message: The Soay sheep case demonstrates that resistance is not "free." High immune responsiveness provides survival advantages during epidemics but incurs costs in survival and reproduction when parasite pressure is low. These costs create a trade-off that maintains genetic variation in resistance within the population. This explains why not all individuals evolve maximum resistance - the optimal level of immune investment depends on the strength and predictability of parasite pressure. The case illustrates balancing selection in action, where environment-dependent fitness effects maintain polymorphism in resistance traits.

Why Don't All Hosts Evolve Maximum Resistance?

Constraints on Evolution of Resistance

Despite strong selection imposed by parasites, complete resistance rarely evolves. Three major factors limit the evolution of resistance:

1. Lack of Genetic Variation

Evolution requires heritable variation in resistance. In some populations, genetic variation may be depleted through:

  • Genetic bottlenecks: Founding events or population crashes reduce genetic diversity
  • Inbreeding: Small or isolated populations lose variation through genetic drift
  • Artificial selection: Selective breeding can reduce variation in immune genes

Example: Tasmanian Devil Facial Tumor Disease

Tasmanian devils have extremely low genetic diversity at MHC loci due to historical population bottlenecks. This "genetic impoverishment has made the immune system blind" to the contagious cancer (Devil Facial Tumor Disease), which is transmitted as an allograft between individuals. The lack of MHC diversity means devils cannot recognize tumor cells as foreign, allowing the cancer to spread unchecked.

Take-home message: Without genetic variation, natural selection cannot act. Conservation of genetic diversity is crucial for populations to adapt to emerging diseases.

2. Host-Parasite Genetic Interactions (Gene-for-Gene)

Resistance is often genetically specific - a host genotype that resists one parasite genotype may be susceptible to another. This creates frequency-dependent selection and coevolutionary dynamics.

Examples of strain-specific immunity:

  • Flax-rust system: Plant resistance genes match specific fungal avirulence genes
  • Daphnia-bacterial parasite: Each host clone is resistant to some parasite strains but susceptible to others
  • Snail-trematode interactions: Matching alleles determine infection success

This genetic specificity means no single "super resistant" genotype exists that can resist all parasite strains. We will explore this coevolutionary dynamic in detail next week.

3. Costs and Trade-offs of Resistance

Resistance mechanisms impose fitness costs that prevent fixation of resistance alleles. These costs operate through several mechanisms:

Metabolic Costs

Maintaining immune defenses requires energy and resources. The immune system is metabolically expensive:

  • Antibody production requires amino acids and energy
  • Fever responses increase metabolic rate by 10-15%
  • Lymphocyte proliferation during infection demands substantial resources

Resource Allocation Trade-off:

Etotal = Egrowth + Ereproduction + Eimmunity + Emaintenance

Where E represents energy allocation to different functions. Energy invested in immunity reduces energy available for growth and reproduction.

Immunopathology

Strong immune responses can cause self-damage:

  • Inflammation damages host tissues alongside parasites
  • Autoimmune reactions occur when immune system attacks self-antigens
  • Cytokine storms can be fatal in severe infections
  • Allergic reactions result from overly aggressive immune responses

Ecological Costs

Resistance alleles may reduce fitness in other ecological contexts:

  • Increased metabolic rate may reduce starvation resistance
  • Immune activation may reduce competitive ability
  • Resistance traits may trade off with other adaptive traits
Key Insight: The existence of costs explains why resistance alleles do not go to fixation. When parasite pressure is high, resistance provides net benefits. When parasite pressure is low, the costs outweigh benefits, and susceptible genotypes have higher fitness. This creates balancing selection that maintains genetic variation in natural populations.

Tolerance as an Alternative Strategy

Resistance vs. Tolerance: A Conceptual Framework

Hosts can respond to parasites through two fundamentally different strategies:

Strategy Mechanism Effect on Parasite Effect on Host Fitness
Resistance Detect and eliminate pathogens Reduces parasite load Reduces infection probability
Tolerance Minimize damage from infection No direct effect Maintains fitness despite infection

Measuring Tolerance

Tolerance is measured as the slope of the relationship between host fitness and parasite load. A shallower (less negative) slope indicates greater tolerance.

Tolerance Reaction Norm:

Fitness = a - b × (Parasite Load)

Where:

a = fitness when uninfected (intercept)

b = rate of fitness decline per unit parasite load (slope)

Tolerance = 1/b

Two genotypes with the same parasite loads but different fitness values differ in tolerance, not resistance.

Mechanisms of Tolerance

  • Tissue repair: Enhanced wound healing and tissue regeneration
  • Anorexia: Reducing feeding during infection limits nutrient availability to parasites
  • Increased resource acquisition: Compensatory feeding or metabolism
  • Stress response regulation: Dampening inflammation to reduce immunopathology
  • Antioxidant production: Neutralizing free radicals produced during infection

Example: Hawaiian Amakihi and Avian Malaria Tolerance

Hawaiian honeycreepers (Amakihi) were historically restricted to high elevations (>1,000m) where mosquito vectors of avian malaria (Plasmodium relictum) were absent. Low-elevation forests had high malaria prevalence, and most native birds died if exposed.

Evidence of Evolution: By the 2000s, Amakihi were observed breeding successfully in low-elevation forests despite high malaria prevalence (72-83%). When researchers experimentally infected birds from low and high elevations:

  • Low-elevation birds had 50% mortality vs. 83% for high-elevation birds
  • Low-elevation birds maintained higher body weight during infection
  • Both groups had similar parasite loads (parasitemia)

This indicates evolution of tolerance rather than resistance - low-elevation birds maintained fitness despite similar parasite burdens.

Take-home message: When resistance is difficult to evolve (due to lack of genetic variation or high costs), tolerance may provide an alternative evolutionary pathway. Tolerance can allow host populations to persist in the presence of virulent parasites without necessarily reducing transmission.

Evolutionary Dynamics of Tolerance vs. Resistance

Tolerance and resistance have different consequences for disease dynamics:

  • Resistance reduces parasite transmission by reducing parasite load in hosts, potentially driving parasite extinction
  • Tolerance does not reduce transmission and may even increase it by allowing hosts to remain active and infectious for longer periods
  • Tolerance may favor evolution of higher virulence in parasites (hosts can tolerate more damage, removing constraint on virulence)
  • Tolerance may be favored when resistance is costly or genetically constrained

Key Takeaways

  1. Hosts can express resistance through multiple mechanisms including innate and adaptive immunity, physical barriers, behavioral avoidance, and lack of susceptible tissues.
  2. Hosts can rapidly evolve resistance when confronted with novel parasites, as demonstrated by the myxomatosis-rabbit system and Hawaiian honeycreeper-malaria system. Evolution can occur on timescales of years to decades.
  3. Resistance is not "free" - costs and trade-offs are associated with all forms of resistance. These costs include metabolic expenses, immunopathology, and reduced fitness in other ecological contexts.
  4. Costs maintain genetic variation through balancing selection. The Soay sheep and sickle cell examples show that resistance alleles may increase fitness in some environments (high parasite pressure) but decrease fitness in others (low parasite pressure).
  5. Gene-for-gene interactions create complex dynamics where no single genotype is universally resistant. This genetic specificity contributes to the maintenance of polymorphism.
  6. Tolerance is an alternative to resistance that allows hosts to maintain fitness despite infection without directly reducing parasite load. Tolerance may be favored when resistance is costly or genetically constrained.
  7. The evolution of resistance and tolerance has implications for disease control. Understanding costs and benefits of different defensive strategies is crucial for predicting host population responses to emerging diseases and designing effective management strategies.
