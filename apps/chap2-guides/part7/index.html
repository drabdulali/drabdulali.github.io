Phase 1 — Interpreting Fixed Effects
01
Odds Ratios from Logistic GLMM
Back-transforming log-odds coefficients to policy-interpretable scale
▼

Input

Fixed-effect coefficient \(\beta_k\) on log-odds scale from fixef(model)

Output

Odds Ratio OR\(_k\) = \(e^{\beta_k}\) with 95% CI on multiplicative scale

Why exponentiate

Log-odds are uninterpretable in isolation. OR is a ratio familiar to clinicians and policymakers — "X-fold change in odds."

Back-Transformation Formula

Odds Ratio from log-odds coefficient
\[ \text{OR}_k = e^{\beta_k} \] \[ \text{95\% CI: } \left( e^{\,\beta_k - 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}_k},\; e^{\,\beta_k + 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}_k} \right) \]
OR > 1
Risk factor — increases persistence odds
OR = 1
No effect — \(\beta = 0\)
OR < 1
Protective — decreases persistence odds

Worked Example

livestock_density: \(\beta = 0.35\), SE = 0.09 OR = \(e^{0.35}\) = 1.42    95% CI: \(e^{0.35 \pm 1.96 \times 0.09}\) = [1.19, 1.69]

Interpretation: "Each 1-SD increase in livestock density is associated with 42% higher odds of persistence (OR = 1.42, 95% CI [1.19–1.69], p < 0.001), after adjusting for other predictors and random effects."

R Extraction Commands

# Point estimates — already on log-odds scale
fixef(model)

# Convert to OR
exp(fixef(model))

# 95% CI on OR scale (profile likelihood)
exp(confint(model, method = "Wald"))

# Full tidy output with OR + CI in one step
broom.mixed::tidy(model, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

02
Hazard Ratios from Cox Frailty Model
Interpreting time-to-reemergence coefficients — faster vs. slower events
▼

Input

\(\beta_k\) from Cox frailty model on log-hazard scale

Output

HR\(_k\) = \(e^{\beta_k}\) — ratio of instantaneous event rates

Why different from OR

OR is a cross-sectional ratio. HR is a rate ratio describing how quickly an event occurs over time — it accounts for the temporal dimension.

Back-Transformation Formula

Hazard Ratio from Cox log-hazard coefficient
\[ \text{HR}_k = e^{\beta_k} \] \[ h(t \mid z_k + 1) = h(t \mid z_k) \times \text{HR}_k \]
HR > 1
Faster reemergence — shorter time to event
HR = 1
No effect on reemergence timing
HR < 1
Delayed reemergence — protective factor

Worked Example

vaccination_coverage: \(\beta = -0.28\), SE = 0.10 HR = \(e^{-0.28}\) = 0.76    95% CI: \(e^{-0.28 \pm 1.96 \times 0.10}\) = [0.62, 0.92]

Interpretation: "Each 1-SD increase in vaccination coverage is associated with a 24% lower hazard of reemergence (HR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.62–0.92], p = 0.006), meaning disease-free areas with higher vaccination take significantly longer to experience a new outbreak."

R Extraction Commands

# Hazard ratios — exp(coef) column in summary
summary(model_cox)$coefficients

# Point estimates only
exp(coef(model_cox))

# 95% CI on HR scale
exp(confint(model_cox))

# Tidy output
broom::tidy(model_cox, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

OR vs. HR: Key Conceptual Difference

FeatureOdds Ratio (OR)Hazard Ratio (HR)
ModelLogistic GLMMCox frailty
Outcome typeBinary (0/1 persistence)Time-to-event (reemergence)
InterpretationRatio of odds of the eventRatio of instantaneous event rates
HR > 1 meansN/AEvent happens sooner (faster reemergence)
OR > 1 meansHigher probability of persistenceN/A
Handles censoringNo (all units have complete outcome)Yes — right-censored units contribute properly
R extractionexp(fixef(model))exp(coef(model_cox))
Common exam trap: HR > 1 is not always bad In persistence, OR > 1 = more disease. In reemergence, HR > 1 means the event (reemergence) happens faster — which is worse for disease control. HR < 1 is the protective direction for reemergence: the area stays disease-free longer. Always anchor interpretation to the specific event being modelled.
Phase 2 — Random Effects
03
Variance Components and ICC
Quantifying how much variation is explained by geographic clustering
▼

Input

VarCorr(model) extracting \(\sigma^2\) at each random effect level

Output

ICC per level — proportion of total variance attributable to each grouping

Why it matters

High ICC means geographic structure dominates. Fixed effects explain less than clustering; policy must address country- or admin-level factors.

ICC Formula for 3-Level Logistic GLMM

Intraclass Correlation Coefficient — logistic model (level-2 = country)
\[ \text{ICC}_{\text{country}} = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{country}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{country}} + \sigma^2_{\text{admin2}} + \pi^2/3} \]

\(\pi^2/3 \approx 3.29\) = variance of the standard logistic distribution (level-1 residual variance)

ICC at admin-2 level (total clustering within country)
\[ \text{ICC}_{\text{admin2}} = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{admin2}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{country}} + \sigma^2_{\text{admin2}} + \pi^2/3} \]

R Extraction

# Extract variance components
vc <- VarCorr(model)
print(vc, comp = "Variance")

# Manual ICC calculation
s2_country <- as.numeric(vc$country)
s2_admin2  <- as.numeric(vc$admin2)
s2_resid   <- (pi^2) / 3          # logistic level-1 variance
total      <- s2_country + s2_admin2 + s2_resid

icc_country <- s2_country / total
icc_admin2  <- s2_admin2  / total

# Or use performance package
performance::icc(model)

What high ICC reveals about your model ICC > 0.20 at country level means you have substantial unmeasured confounding operating at the country scale (e.g. surveillance capacity, policy quality, reporting standards). This does not invalidate fixed effect estimates — the random intercepts absorb this — but it does mean your predictors alone cannot account for country-to-country differences.
04
Frailty Variance (θ) in the Cox Model
Quantifying unmeasured heterogeneity in reemergence timing across admin-2 units
▼

Input

Fitted Cox frailty model — coxme() or coxph() with frailty()

Output

\(\theta\) = variance of the log-normal frailty; individual frailty scores \(b_j\)

Why frailty

Admin-2 units are not exchangeable — some areas consistently re-experience disease faster due to unmeasured local factors. Frailty captures this.

Frailty Distribution

Gamma frailty — multiplicative random effect on hazard
\[ h(t \mid \mathbf{z}_j, b_j) = h_0(t) \cdot b_j \cdot \exp(\mathbf{x}_j^{\top} \boldsymbol{\beta}) \] \[ b_j \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\frac{1}{\theta}, \frac{1}{\theta}\right), \quad \mathbb{E}[b_j] = 1, \quad \text{Var}(b_j) = \theta \]

R Extraction

# For coxph() with frailty() term
model_cox$history      # convergence details
model_cox$frail          # individual log-frailty estimates per unit
model_cox$history[["frailty(admin2)"]]$theta   # variance theta

# For coxme() — mixed effects Cox
VarCorr(model_coxme)     # frailty variance
ranef(model_coxme)       # individual frailty estimates

# Summarise frailty distribution
frail_scores <- exp(model_cox$frail)  # back to multiplicative scale
summary(frail_scores)
hist(frail_scores, breaks = 30)

Interpreting θ

\(\theta\) valueInterpretationPolicy implication
\(\theta \approx 0\)Negligible frailty — all admin-2 units have similar re-emergence ratesFixed predictors explain most variation; uniform policy may work
\(\theta = 0.2\text{–}0.4\)Moderate heterogeneity — meaningful clustering existsSome geographic targeting of interventions warranted
\(\theta > 0.5\)Substantial heterogeneity — large unexplained variation in timingStrong case for place-based interventions; identify high-frailty units
Reporting template for \(\theta = 0.45\) "The frailty variance was \(\theta\) = 0.45, indicating substantial unmeasured heterogeneity in reemergence timing across admin-2 units. This suggests local factors not captured in the fixed-effect predictors (e.g. live animal trade patterns, veterinary response capacity) contribute substantially to variation in time to reemergence."

H4 — Wildlife × Tree Cover Interaction

H4 The effect of wildlife proximity on persistence is moderated by tree cover (forest amplifies risk) Partial

An interaction term \(\beta_{\text{wildlife} \times \text{tree}}\) tests whether the slope of wildlife_proximity on log-odds differs as a function of tree_cover. A positive interaction coefficient (\(\beta > 0\)) means wildlife risk is amplified in forested areas.

Interaction term in the linear predictor
\[ \eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1 z_{\text{wildlife}} + \beta_2 z_{\text{tree}} + \beta_3 \cdot (z_{\text{wildlife}} \times z_{\text{tree}}) + \ldots \]

The marginal effect of wildlife_proximity at tree cover value \(t\): \(\;\partial\eta/\partial z_{\text{wildlife}} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 \cdot t\)

Plotting Marginal Effects (R)

# Visualise interaction with interact_plot()
interactions::interact_plot(
  model,
  pred        = wildlife_proximity,
  modx        = tree_cover,
  modx.values = "plus-minus",   # -1SD, mean, +1SD of tree_cover
  interval    = TRUE,
  x.label     = "Wildlife Proximity (z-score)",
  y.label     = "Predicted Persistence Probability",
  legend.main = "Tree Cover Level"
)

# Compute marginal effects at specific values
marginaleffects::plot_slopes(
  model,
  variables  = "wildlife_proximity",
  condition  = "tree_cover"
)
Interpreting a partial support decision for H4 If \(\beta_3 = 0.19\) (p = 0.04): the interaction is statistically significant. The marginal effect of wildlife proximity is +0.41 at low tree cover but +0.60 at high tree cover. Report: "The wildlife–persistence association was stronger in densely forested districts (\(\beta_{\text{interaction}}\) = 0.19, p = 0.04), providing partial support for H4. However, the interaction explained only 2% of additional deviance, suggesting tree cover amplifies but does not drive the wildlife effect."

H5 — Meta-Regression Moderators

H5 Cross-TAD heterogeneity in OR estimates is explained by disease-specific moderators (e.g. transmission route) Partial

H5 uses meta-regression via metafor to test whether between-TAD heterogeneity in the livestock density OR is moderated by disease characteristics (e.g. direct vs. vector-borne transmission).

# Fit meta-regression with moderator
res <- metafor::rma(
  yi   = log_OR,           # log-OR from each TAD-specific model
  sei  = se_log_OR,
  mods = ~ transmission_route + host_range,
  data = tad_results
)

# Heterogeneity statistics
res$QE   # Cochran's Q (residual heterogeneity)
res$QEp  # p-value for Q
res$I2   # I² — % variance due to heterogeneity
res$tau2  # tau² — between-study variance

# Moderator significance
res$QM   # Q_M statistic for moderators
res$QMp  # p-value for moderator block
H5 Reporting Template "Substantial heterogeneity was observed across TADs (I² = 63%, \(\tau^2\) = 0.12, Q = 38.2, df = 14, p < 0.001). Meta-regression identified transmission route as a significant moderator (\(Q_M\) = 9.4, df = 2, p = 0.009): direct-contact diseases showed larger livestock density effects (pooled log-OR = 0.48) compared to vector-borne diseases (pooled log-OR = 0.21). This provides partial support for H5, accounting for 38% of between-TAD variance in the livestock density effect."

08
Effect Size Interpretation Guide
Standardised thresholds for OR and HR in epidemiology, with Cohen's d equivalents
▼

Context

OR and HR are dimensionless ratios. "How big is big?" depends on the literature and outcome rarity. Use these thresholds as a starting point.

For risk factors (OR/HR > 1)

1.0–1.5 = small   1.5–3.0 = moderate   >3.0 = large

For protective factors (OR/HR < 1)

0.5–1.0 = small protective   <0.5 = large protective effect

Risk-Factor Thresholds (OR or HR > 1)

1.0–1.5
Small
Modest elevation in odds or hazard. May be epidemiologically meaningful if exposure is widespread. Cohen's d ≈ 0.20.
1.5–3.0
Moderate
Clear substantive effect. Likely to influence policy decisions. Comparable to Cohen's d ≈ 0.50–0.80.
> 3.0
Large
Strong risk factor. Comparable to Cohen's d > 1.0. Often seen for direct transmission pathways or very low baseline rates.

Protective-Factor Thresholds (OR or HR < 1)

0.5–1.0
Small Protective
Meaningful but modest protection. Vaccination at standard coverage levels often falls here (OR ≈ 0.6–0.8).
< 0.5
Large Protective
Halving (or more) of risk. Rare in observational studies due to residual confounding. Corresponds to Cohen's d > 0.80.

Cohen's d to OR Conversion

Approximate conversion (assuming logistic distribution)
\[ d \approx \frac{\ln(\text{OR})}{\pi / \sqrt{3}} = \frac{\ln(\text{OR})}{1.814} \]

d = 0.2 ⇒ OR ≈ 1.44    d = 0.5 ⇒ OR ≈ 2.48    d = 0.8 ⇒ OR ≈ 4.13

OR approximates RR only for rare outcomes When outcome prevalence > 10%, OR overestimates the relative risk. For TAD persistence (common in high-burden regions), the OR may substantially exceed the true risk ratio. Use the formula \(\text{RR} \approx \text{OR} / [(1 - P_0) + P_0 \times \text{OR}]\) to convert, where \(P_0\) is baseline prevalence in unexposed group. Always report OR (the model's native output) and note this limitation.
Context is king — benchmarks for TAD persistence studies In animal disease epidemiology, ORs of 1.5–2.5 for ecological predictors are typical. ORs > 3.0 often indicate either a very specific mechanistic pathway (e.g. direct wildlife-livestock contact) or potential residual confounding. Always compare effect sizes to the biological plausibility of the underlying mechanism.
