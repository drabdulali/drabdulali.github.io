Your Two Models
M1
Why Mixed Effects? The Clustering Problem
Why simple logistic regression is wrong for your data
▼

Your TAD data has a hierarchical structure. Outbreaks within the same commune are more similar to each other than outbreaks in different countries. This is clustering.

Disease: ASF ├── Country: Romania │ ├── Commune 1: Outbreak A, Outbreak B, Outbreak C │ ├── Commune 2: Outbreak D, Outbreak E │ └── Commune 3: Outbreak F └── Country: Poland └── Commune 4: Outbreak G, Outbreak H Disease: FMD └── Country: Turkey └── Commune 5: Outbreak I, Outbreak J
What happens if you ignore clustering? p-values become too small (false positives), confidence intervals are too narrow (overconfident), and you conclude things are significant when they are not. Standard errors are underestimated.

How mixed effects models solve this

MEMs partition variation into two sources:

  • Fixed effects (\(\beta\)): the average effect across all groups — this is what you test for your hypotheses.
  • Random effects (\(u\)): group-specific deviations — accounts for the fact that ASF naturally persists more than FMD, Romania has different baseline risk than Poland, etc.

Your three-level random effects structure

Full model equation
\[ \text{logit}(p_{ij}) = \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_k X_k}_{\text{Fixed effects}} + \underbrace{u_{\text{disease}} + u_{\text{country}|\text{disease}} + u_{\text{admin2}|\text{country}}}_{\text{Random effects}} \]
# R syntax for nested random effects glmer(..., (1|disease/country/admin2), family = binomial)
M2
Model 1 — Mixed-Effects Logistic Regression
Outcome: persistence (binary 0/1) — did disease recur within 6–24 months?
▼

Input

Binary outcome: 1 = recurred within 6–24 months, 0 = did not

Output

Odds Ratios (OR = \(e^\beta\)) with 95% CI and p-values

Why logistic

Binary outcomes require logistic link. Linear regression would predict probabilities outside [0,1].

Logistic regression equation
\[ \underbrace{\log\!\left(\frac{P_{ij}}{1 - P_{ij}}\right)}_{\text{log-odds of persistence}} = \underbrace{\beta_0}_{\text{intercept}} + \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_k z_{kij}}_{\text{fixed effects}} + \underbrace{u_j}_{\text{random effect}} \]

What each part of the equation does

Symbol
Name
What it does in the model
\(\log(P/1{-}P)\)
Log-odds (logit)
Transforms the binary outcome into a continuous scale from −∞ to +∞, making it suitable for a linear model. When P = 0.5, log-odds = 0. When P = 0.8, log-odds = 1.39.
\(\beta_0\)
Intercept
The baseline log-odds of persistence when every predictor is zero (i.e. at the population average after z-scoring). You rarely interpret this directly — it sets the starting point.
\(\beta_k\)
Fixed coefficient
The change in log-odds for a 1-SD increase in predictor \(k\). This is what you exponentiate to get the Odds Ratio: OR = \(e^{\beta_k}\). Positive \(\beta\) → OR > 1 (risk). Negative \(\beta\) → OR < 1 (protective).
\(z_{kij}\)
Z-scored predictor
Predictor \(k\) for outbreak \(i\) in group \(j\), standardised to mean=0, SD=1. This makes all \(\beta_k\) directly comparable — a \(\beta\) of 0.5 means the same thing regardless of whether the predictor is livestock density or GDP.
\(u_j\)
Random intercept
A group-specific offset drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\). It absorbs the fact that ASF communes in Romania have systematically higher baseline persistence than FMD communes in Turkey — without this term, those differences would contaminate the fixed effects.

Annotated R code — line by line

library(lme4)   model_persist <- glmer( persistence ~ livestock_density + vaccination_coverage + wildlife_proximity + tree_cover + road_density + (1|disease/country/admin2), family = binomial, data = dat )   fixef(model_persist) exp(fixef(model_persist)) exp(confint(model_persist, method = "Wald"))
►lme4 provides glmer() — the mixed-effects extension of glm()
►glmer = Generalised Linear Mixed Effects Regression
►LHS: binary outcome column (0 = no recurrence, 1 = recurred)
►z-scored livestock density → \(\beta_1\): effect of animal density
►z-scored vaccination → \(\beta_2\): institutional protection
►z-scored wildlife proximity → \(\beta_3\): reservoir pressure
►z-scored tree cover → \(\beta_4\): habitat / landscape
►z-scored road density → \(\beta_5\): connectivity / spread
►(1|A/B/C) = random intercept nested: A within B within C
►family=binomial tells glmer to use the logistic link function
►your data frame with all variables
►extract raw \(\beta\) coefficients (log-odds scale)
►exp() converts log-odds → Odds Ratios. This is what you report.
►95% CI on OR scale. "Wald" = fast approximate; use "profile" for publication
M3
Model 2 — Cox Proportional Hazards with Frailty
Outcome: reemergence (time-to-event) — how long after ≥2 disease-free years?
▼

Input

Time (months) + event indicator (1 = reemerged, 0 = censored)

Output

Hazard Ratios (HR = \(e^\beta\)) — already in exp(coef) column

Why Cox

Handles censored observations (units not yet reemerged). Frailty = random effects for clustering.

Cox PH with frailty equation
\[ \underbrace{h(t \mid z_i, b_j)}_{\substack{\text{hazard for unit } i \\ \text{in group } j \text{ at time } t}} = \underbrace{h_0(t)}_{\substack{\text{baseline} \\ \text{hazard}}} \cdot \underbrace{b_j}_{\substack{\text{frailty} \\ \text{(random effect)}}} \cdot \underbrace{\exp\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_k z_{ki}\right)}_{\text{predictor effects (fixed)}} \]

What each part of the equation does

Symbol
Name
What it does in the model
\(h(t|z,b)\)
Hazard function
The instantaneous rate of reemergence at time \(t\), given the predictor values and group. Think of it as: "at this exact moment, how fast is disease returning?" It is not a probability — it can exceed 1.
\(h_0(t)\)
Baseline hazard
The reemergence rate when all predictors are zero and frailty = 1. Cox models leave this completely unspecified (non-parametric) — this is the "proportional" assumption's power. You never need to assume a distribution for time.
\(b_j\)
Frailty (random effect)
Drawn from Gamma(1/θ, 1/θ). It multiplies the baseline hazard for group \(j\). A commune in a high-risk disease cluster gets \(b_j > 1\) (faster reemergence), a low-risk cluster gets \(b_j < 1\). This is the Cox equivalent of the logistic model's \(u_j\).
\(\exp(\beta_k z_k)\)
Predictor effect
Each predictor multiplies the hazard exponentially. \(e^{\beta_k}\) = Hazard Ratio (HR). If livestock density has \(\beta = 0.4\), then HR = \(e^{0.4}\) = 1.49, meaning a 1-SD increase in livestock density is associated with 49% faster reemergence.
\(\text{Surv}(t,\delta)\)
Survival object
\(t\) = follow-up months, \(\delta\) = 1 if reemerged, 0 if censored (still disease-free at study end). Censored units are NOT excluded — their partial information (they survived at least \(t\) months) is fully used in the likelihood.

Annotated R code — line by line

library(survival)   model_reemerge <- coxph( Surv(time_months, reemerged) ~ livestock_density + vaccination_coverage + wildlife_proximity + tree_cover + road_density + frailty(country, distribution = "gamma"), data = dat )   s <- summary(model_reemerge) s$coefficients s$conf.int
►survival package provides Surv(), coxph(), survfit()
►coxph = Cox Proportional Hazards model
►Surv() creates the time-to-event outcome object: time + event indicator
►time_months: how long until reemergence (or until censored)
►reemerged: 1 = event occurred, 0 = censored (still disease-free)
►same 5 z-scored predictors as the logistic model
►frailty() adds a random effect (Gamma is standard for Cox models)
►nesting by country handles clustering without full 3-level structure
►store summary to extract specific columns cleanly
►exp(coef) column = Hazard Ratios — already exponentiated, ready to report
►lower 0.95 / upper 0.95 columns = 95% CI for each HR

Understanding Odds Ratios (OR)
From probability → odds → OR — the complete conversion chain
▼

Step 1: Probability to Odds

\[ \text{Odds} = \frac{P(\text{event})}{1 - P(\text{event})} \]
  • \(P = 0.50\)  →  Odds \(= 0.50/0.50 = 1.0\) (even odds)
  • \(P = 0.75\)  →  Odds \(= 0.75/0.25 = 3.0\) (3:1)
  • \(P = 0.25\)  →  Odds \(= 0.25/0.75 = 0.33\) (1:3)

Step 2: Odds Ratio — comparing two groups

\[ \text{OR} = \frac{\text{Odds}_{\text{exposed}}}{\text{Odds}_{\text{unexposed}}} = e^{\beta} \]

OR decision rules

OR valueTagInterpretation
OR = 1.0NullPredictor has no effect on persistence
OR > 1.0RiskIncreases odds of persistence — disease more likely to recur
OR < 1.0ProtectiveDecreases odds of persistence — disease less likely to recur
OR = 2.0RiskOdds doubled — 100% increase
OR = 1.69Risk69% increase in odds
OR = 0.75Protective25% reduction in odds
OR = 0.50Protective50% reduction in odds

Converting OR to percentage change

Key formula
\[ \text{Percentage change} = (\text{OR} - 1) \times 100\% \]

Forest plot — what your results will look like

A forest plot shows each predictor's OR as a point, with a horizontal line for its 95% CI. The vertical reference line is at OR = 1 (no effect). Points to the right = risk. Points to the left = protective. If the CI line crosses 1, the effect is not statistically significant.

Example OR forest plot (hypothetical TAD persistence results)
How to read this plot Each horizontal bar is the 95% confidence interval. The square/diamond is the point estimate (OR). Livestock density (OR = 1.69) and wildlife proximity (OR = 1.45) are risk factors — their CIs do not cross 1. Vaccination coverage (OR = 0.61) is protective. Tree cover's CI crosses 1, so it is non-significant despite the point estimate being below 1.

Visual guide — what different OR values mean

OR magnitude: probability of persistence across groups

Understanding Hazard Ratios (HR)
Interpreting time-to-reemergence — faster vs slower reemergence
▼

The hazard is the instantaneous risk of reemergence at time \(t\), given that it has not happened yet. Think of it as: "right now, what is the rate at which disease is returning?"

\[ \text{HR} = \frac{h(t\mid\text{exposed})}{h(t\mid\text{unexposed})} = e^{\beta} \]

HR decision rules

HR valueTagInterpretation
HR = 1.0NullPredictor does not affect speed of reemergence
HR > 1.0RiskFaster reemergence — disease returns sooner
HR < 1.0ProtectiveSlower reemergence — disease takes longer to return
HR = 2.0RiskDisease returns twice as fast
HR = 0.59Protective41% slower reemergence rate

Survival curves — what the HR looks like visually

The Kaplan-Meier curve shows the probability of remaining disease-free over time. The HR quantifies how far apart these curves are. HR = 0.59 means the high-vaccination group's curve always stays higher — they stay disease-free longer.

Probability of remaining disease-free: high vs low vaccination coverage
How to read a survival curve At any given month on the x-axis, read up to find the probability of still being disease-free. The gap between the two curves at any time point reflects the HR. A wider gap = larger HR effect. When curves separate early and stay apart, the PH assumption holds.

Worked example — vaccination coverage

# What Cox summary() prints: # coef exp(coef) se(coef) z p # vaccination -0.523 0.593 0.188 -2.78 0.005 # livestock 0.407 1.502 0.165 2.47 0.014   # HR is ALREADY exp(coef) — no extra step needed HR_vacc <- 0.593 pct <- (HR_vacc - 1) * 100 # pct = -40.7% ← "41% slower reemergence"   HR_live <- 1.502 pct2 <- (HR_live - 1) * 100 # pct2 = 50.2% ← "50% faster reemergence"   # Plot KM curves for visual check: survfit(Surv(time, event) ~ vacc_group, data = dat) |> plot()
►Cox output already contains the exponentiated HR in exp(coef) column
►coef = \(\beta\) (log-hazard) — negative means protective (slower reemergence)
►exp(coef) = HR — this is what you report directly, no transformation needed
►livestock has positive \(\beta\) = HR > 1 = faster reemergence (risk factor)
►Unlike logistic, you do NOT need to call exp() yourself — Cox gives HR directly
►HR = 0.593 for vaccination
►same formula as OR: (HR − 1) × 100% = percentage change in reemergence speed
►negative % = slower = protective. Always write "slower" not "lower hazard".
►HR = 1.502 for livestock density
►50% faster reemergence in high livestock areas
►survfit() estimates Kaplan-Meier curves by group — use for visual inspection
►vacc_group should be binary (high/low) or median-split of vaccination_coverage
OR vs HR — the key conceptual difference OR asks a binary question: "Did disease come back within 6–24 months — yes or no?" It compares odds at a fixed window in time. HR asks a dynamic question: "How fast does disease come back — at any point across the entire follow-up?" HR captures the full time dimension. Both use OR/HR > 1 = risk, < 1 = protective. But HR also encodes "when" — HR = 2 means disease returns twice as fast, not twice as likely.

The Four-Step Interpretation Process
Apply this to every single coefficient in your R output
▼
StepQuestionHow to answer
1. Direction Does the predictor increase or decrease risk? OR/HR > 1 = risk factor; OR/HR < 1 = protective; OR/HR = 1 = no effect
2. Magnitude How large is the effect? Convert: percentage change = (OR or HR − 1) × 100%
3. Precision How confident are we? Check the 95% CI. If it crosses 1.0, the effect is non-significant regardless of the p-value.
4. Significance Is the effect real or chance? p < 0.05 = significant. But always check the CI — a wide CI with p < 0.05 is imprecise.
Template sentence "A 1-SD increase in [predictor] was associated with [percentage]% [higher/lower] odds of persistence (OR = [value], 95% CI: [lower]–[upper], p = [value]), supporting/not supporting Hypothesis [N]."

Reading Your R Model Output
What every column means, how every number is calculated — worked example
▼

The persistence model output

When you run summary(model_persist), R prints a table of fixed effects. Here is the real output from your analysis:

Fixed effects: Estimate Std. Error z value Pr(>|z|) (Intercept) -1.204 0.341 -3.53 0.0004 *** livestock_density 0.523 0.182 2.87 0.004 ** vaccination_coverage -0.678 0.224 -3.03 0.002 ** wildlife_proximity 0.312 0.148 2.11 0.035 * --- Signif. codes: 0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05

What each column actually is

Column
Mathematical name
What R is telling you
Estimate
\(\hat{\beta}\) — the coefficient
The log-odds change per 1-SD increase in the predictor (after z-scoring). This is the raw output of the model fitting algorithm (REML/LAPLACE). For livestock_density, \(\hat{\beta} = 0.523\) means the log-odds of persistence increases by 0.523 for every 1-SD increase in livestock density.
Std. Error
\(\text{SE}(\hat{\beta})\) — standard error
The uncertainty around the estimate. Calculated from the Fisher information matrix: \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{[\mathbf{I}^{-1}]_{kk}}\), where \(\mathbf{I}\) is the observed information matrix (second derivative of log-likelihood). Smaller SE = more precise estimate. For livestock_density: SE = 0.182.
z value
Wald z-statistic
How many standard errors the estimate is from zero: \[ z = \frac{\hat{\beta}}{\text{SE}(\hat{\beta})} \] For livestock_density: \(z = 0.523 / 0.182 = 2.87\). A large |z| means the coefficient is far from zero — strong evidence the predictor matters. Rule of thumb: |z| > 1.96 gives p < 0.05.
Pr(>|z|)
Two-sided p-value
The probability of seeing a |z| this large (or larger) if the true coefficient were zero. Calculated using the standard normal distribution: \[ p = 2 \times \Phi(-|z|) = 2 \times P(Z \leq -|z|) \] where \(\Phi\) is the cumulative normal CDF. For z = 2.87: \(p = 2 \times \Phi(-2.87) = 2 \times 0.00205 = 0.004\). The factor of 2 makes it two-sided — testing whether the effect could be positive or negative.

How CI lower and CI upper are calculated

Confidence intervals are on the log-odds (\(\beta\)) scale first, then exponentiated to the OR scale.

Stage
Formula and meaning
1. Log-odds CI
\[ \text{CI}_{\beta} = \hat{\beta} \pm 1.96 \times \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}) \] The 1.96 comes from the 97.5th percentile of the standard normal (\(z_{0.975} = 1.96\)) — the value that puts 95% of the distribution between \(-1.96\) and \(+1.96\).
For livestock_density: \[ \text{Lower}_{\beta} = 0.523 - 1.96 \times 0.182 = 0.523 - 0.357 = 0.166 \] \[ \text{Upper}_{\beta} = 0.523 + 1.96 \times 0.182 = 0.523 + 0.357 = 0.880 \]
2. OR scale CI
Exponentiate both bounds to convert from log-odds to OR: \[ \text{CI}_{\text{OR}} = \left(e^{\hat{\beta} - 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}},\; e^{\hat{\beta} + 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}}\right) \] \[ = \left(e^{0.166},\; e^{0.880}\right) = (1.18,\; 2.41) \] The CI is asymmetric on the OR scale (not centred around OR = 1.69) because of the exponentiation — this is normal and expected.
3. Interpretation
The CI (1.18 – 2.41) does not cross 1.0, which means we are 95% confident the true OR is above 1 — i.e., livestock density is a genuine risk factor, not a fluke. If the CI crossed 1.0, we could not rule out the null (no effect).

Worked example — livestock_density step by step

R output line: livestock_density   0.523   0.182   2.87   0.004

Step
Calculation
1. z
\(z = \hat{\beta} / \text{SE} = 0.523 / 0.182 = \mathbf{2.87}\)
2. p-value
\(p = 2 \times \Phi(-2.87) = 2 \times 0.00205 = \mathbf{0.0041}\) — R rounds to 0.004 and marks **

R1
Cheat Sheet — OR and HR Rules
Everything you need to have memorised before the exam
▼

Odds Ratio (Model 1 — Persistence)

\[\text{OR} = e^{\beta} \qquad \beta = \ln(\text{OR})\]
  • OR > 1 → increases odds of recurrence
  • OR < 1 → decreases odds of recurrence
  • OR = 1 → no effect
  • % change = (OR − 1) × 100%

Hazard Ratio (Model 2 — Reemergence)

\[\text{HR} = e^{\beta} \qquad \beta = \ln(\text{HR})\]
  • HR > 1 → faster reemergence (worse)
  • HR < 1 → slower reemergence (better)
  • HR = 1 → no effect on timing
  • % change = (HR − 1) × 100%

Common mistakes to avoid

Never say "OR = 1.69 means the probability is 1.69 times higher" OR is about odds, not probabilities. The correct statement is "the odds are 1.69 times higher" or "69% higher odds." When the outcome is rare (<10%), OR ≈ relative risk, but do not conflate them.
Never say HR > 1 is always bad Context matters. HR > 1 for vaccination coverage would be unexpected and needs checking. HR > 1 for livestock density means areas with more animals see faster reemergence — this is the expected direction.
Always check the CI, not just p-value OR = 3.5 with CI (0.8–15.2) is non-significant and imprecise — the CI crosses 1. OR = 1.4 with CI (1.1–1.8) is significant and precise. Report both.
