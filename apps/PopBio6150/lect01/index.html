Foundations

What is disease ecology?

Ecology studies interactions among organisms and their environment; disease ecology applies this to pathogens. For parasites, the host (and host population/community plus abiotic context) is the environment in which infection, transmission, and evolution unfold. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

  • Microparasites (viruses, bacteria, protozoa): binary infection status, rapid replication, immunity common.
  • Macroparasites (worms, ectoparasites): burden matters; partial immunity; aggregation common.

Why it matters

  • Human & wildlife health, conservation: pathogens can drive declines/extinctions; management needs ecology. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}
  • Multi-host reality: many pathogens bridge wildlife–livestock–human networks. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}
  • Anthropogenic change: land use, urbanization, and climate reshape contact and suitability. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

Case studies

Hendra virus (Australia)

  • RNA virus shed in bat urine; benign in bats, severe in horses/dogs/humans. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}
  • Urban fruit trees & permanent bat camps near people ↑ contact & spillover risk. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}
  • Principles: habitat disruption → novel contact networks; multi-host spillover; modeling clarifies dynamics. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

Avian malaria (Hawaii)

  • Pre-1830: no mosquitoes → later introductions + Plasmodium relictum → honeycreeper collapses. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}
  • Native birds highly susceptible (65–90% mortality); introduced birds can amplify. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}
  • Warming shifts vector/parasite to higher elevations; protection of high-elevation forests is crucial. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}
  • Rapid evolution: low-elevation Amakihi show tolerance within ~100 years. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

Measles (humans)

  • Highly contagious, lifelong immunity post-infection. :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}
  • Critical community size ≈ 300k–500k for persistence; small populations show fade-outs. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}
  • Biennial cycles & strong seasonality tied to school terms (“pulses”). :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}
  • Vaccination can interrupt transmission and eliminate outbreaks. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}

The big picture

  • Density & persistence: higher host density → easier pathogen persistence. :contentReference[oaicite:19]{index=19}
  • Contact structure: social behavior (schools, farms, urban wildlife) shapes β and superspreading. :contentReference[oaicite:20]{index=20}
  • Seasonal forcing: periodic changes in contact/susceptibles sustain cycles. :contentReference[oaicite:21]{index=21}
  • Multi-host landscapes & reservoirs complicate control. :contentReference[oaicite:22]{index=22}
  • Global change: climate & land use shift vector suitability and encounter rates. :contentReference[oaicite:23]{index=23}

Summary

  • Pathogens live in ecological networks: the host is their environment, but population structure and climate set the stage. :contentReference[oaicite:26]{index=26}
  • For microparasites, R₀, contact rates, and susceptible supply (births/waning) govern growth and persistence. :contentReference[oaicite:27]{index=27}
  • Thresholds matter: critical community size separates persistence from fade-out; vaccination shifts effective R0 below 1. :contentReference[oaicite:28]{index=28}
  • Multi-host realities and environmental change (urbanization, climate) reshape spillover risks (Hendra; Hawaiian birds). :contentReference[oaicite:29]{index=29} :contentReference[oaicite:30]{index=30}
  • Management leverages ecology: reduce contact, protect refugia, vaccinate strategically, and monitor reservoirs.
