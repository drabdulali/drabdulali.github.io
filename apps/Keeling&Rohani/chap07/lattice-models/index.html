Introduction: Understanding Spatial Structure in Disease Spread

Imagine you're watching a forest fire spread from tree to tree, or tracking how a cold moves through a classroom. In both cases, location matters. The disease doesn't jump randomly across the population; it spreads from neighbor to neighbor. This is exactly what lattice-based models help us understand.

Made Simple

Lattice-based models arrange individuals (or groups) in a grid pattern, like a checkerboard. Instead of assuming everyone can infect everyone else equally (which is what simpler models assume), these models recognize that you're more likely to infect your neighbors than someone far away.

Think of it like living in an apartment building. You're much more likely to catch a cold from the person living next door than from someone three floors up.

Why Spatial Structure Matters

In traditional well-mixed models (like basic SIR models), we assume any infected person can contact any susceptible person with equal probability. But real populations have spatial structure:

  • People interact more with those nearby (neighbors, classmates, coworkers)
  • Diseases spread locally before spreading globally
  • Local depletion of susceptibles slows down transmission
  • Spatial barriers can create isolation and prevent spread

7.3.2 Cellular Automata: The Building Blocks

Cellular automata are a special type of lattice model where each grid square (or "cell") holds just one individual. Each cell can be in one of a few simple states, like susceptible, infectious, or recovered. The magic happens when we apply simple rules about how the disease spreads from cell to cell.

Key Features of Cellular Automata

  • Grid structure: Individuals sit on a fixed lattice (like squares on graph paper)
  • Limited states: Each cell can only be in a few states (e.g., Empty, Susceptible, Infectious, Recovered)
  • Local interactions: Usually only the nearest 4 or 8 neighbors can interact
  • Stochastic rules: Transitions happen randomly based on probabilities

While real populations don't actually sit in perfect grids, these models teach us how spatial structure affects disease spread. They're abstract tools that reveal important patterns.

Real-World Parallels

Agricultural Settings: In crop fields or livestock farms, individuals do exist in relatively fixed spatial arrangements. Disease spread in these systems closely follows cellular automaton dynamics.

Human Populations: While humans move around, we still have "neighborhoods" of interaction - our homes, workplaces, schools. Cellular automata help us understand the basic principles of local transmission.

7.3.2.1 The Contact Process: A Spatial SIS Model

Made Simple

The contact process is the simplest spatial disease model. Think of a line of people standing next to each other. When someone is infectious, they can only spread the disease to the person immediately to their left or right. After some time, they recover and become susceptible again, so the disease can hit them again later. This is an SIS (Susceptible-Infectious-Susceptible) model in space.

The Basic Rules

If you're Infectious (I):

• You transmit to neighboring susceptibles at rate $\tau$

• You recover (become S again) at rate $\gamma$

Understanding the States

Let's visualize the possible transitions in one dimension. Each person has two neighbors (left and right):

Transmission Events:

$$[S] - [I] - [S] \xrightarrow{\tau} [I] - [I] - [S]$$

$$[S] - [S] - [I] \xrightarrow{\tau} [S] - [I] - [I]$$

$$[I] - [S] - [I] \xrightarrow{2\tau} [I] - [I] - [I]$$


Recovery Event:

$$[A] - [I] - [B] \xrightarrow{\gamma} [A] - [S] - [B]$$

where A and B can be either S or I

Notice how the threshold $\tau/\gamma \approx 1.65$ is needed for persistence, much higher than the naive $R_0 = 2(\tau/\gamma) = 1$ would suggest.

Why R₀ is Different in Spatial Models

In a well-mixed population (non-spatial), we calculate $R_0$ as: number of contacts × transmission probability × infectious period. For the contact process, this would give us:

$$R_0 = 2 \times \frac{\tau}{\gamma}$$

where 2 = number of neighbors in 1D

This would suggest that the disease can spread if $\tau > \frac{1}{2}\gamma$. However, this is wrong because it ignores spatial structure.

The Spatial Problem: Local Depletion

Imagine you're the first infected person in a line. You infect your neighbor to the right. But now that neighbor only has ONE susceptible neighbor left (you've already recovered or are immune). The pool of available susceptibles around them has been depleted. This slows down the spread dramatically.

Through extensive computer simulations, scientists found the true threshold is:

$$\tau > 1.64896\gamma \text{ for disease to persist}$$

This is more than 3 times higher than the simple $R_0$ calculation suggested! Spatial structure makes it much harder for diseases to spread.

7.3.2.2 The Forest-Fire Model: A Spatial SIRS Model

Made Simple

The forest-fire model gets its name from forest ecology, but it works perfectly for diseases. Imagine a forest of trees (susceptibles). Occasionally, lightning strikes and sets a tree on fire (imports infection). Fire spreads to neighboring trees (transmission). Burned areas become empty (recovered/immune). Over time, new trees grow in the empty spaces (birth of new susceptibles or waning immunity).

The Rules in Disease Terms

$$S \xrightarrow{\tau n + \varepsilon} I$$

where $n$ = number of infectious neighbors

$\varepsilon$ = random import of infection (the "lightning")


$$I \xrightarrow{\gamma} R \quad \text{(recovery)}$$
$$R \xrightarrow{\nu} S \quad \text{(birth or waning immunity)}$$

Step-by-Step: What Each Parameter Means

  • $\tau$ (tau): Transmission rate per infectious contact. Higher $\tau$ means disease spreads faster between neighbors.
  • $n$: Number of infectious neighbors (typically 4 or 8 on a 2D grid)
  • $\varepsilon$ (epsilon): Rate of random infection from outside. This is usually very small, representing occasional travelers bringing disease in.
  • $\gamma$ (gamma): Recovery rate. Higher $\gamma$ means shorter infectious period.
  • $\nu$ (nu): Rate at which recovered individuals become susceptible again (birth or immunity loss).

The Magic: Power Laws and Self-Organized Criticality

When we run this model with realistic parameters, something remarkable happens. The model naturally organizes itself into a special state where epidemic sizes follow a power-law distribution.

What is a Power Law?

A power law means that the frequency of epidemics decreases as a power of their size. Mathematically:

$$P(\text{epidemic} \geq s) = s^{-\alpha}$$

In plain English: Small outbreaks are common, medium outbreaks are less common, and large outbreaks are rare, but this relationship follows a specific mathematical pattern. There's no "typical" outbreak size - you can get epidemics of many different scales.

When Do Power Laws Emerge?

Power laws appear when the rates follow a natural ordering found in most real diseases:

$$\tau \gg \gamma \gg \nu \gg \varepsilon$$

(Transmission $\gg$ Recovery $\gg$ Births $\gg$ Imports)

Real-World Example: Measles

For measles, this ordering makes perfect sense:

  • Transmission ($\tau$): Very fast - if you're near an infectious person, you'll likely catch it within days
  • Recovery ($\gamma$): Happens over 1-2 weeks
  • Births ($\nu$): New susceptible children are born over months/years
  • Imports ($\varepsilon$): Rare - occasional traveler brings disease back to a cleared population

This natural separation of timescales leads to the power-law behavior we observe in real epidemic data.

7.3.2.3 Application: Power Laws in Childhood Epidemic Data

Real Data from the Faroe Islands

The Faroe Islands are a small group of islands in the North Atlantic (between Scotland and Iceland) with about 25,000 people. They're perfect for studying epidemics because:

  • They're isolated (limited imports of disease)
  • They have excellent historical health records going back 100+ years
  • The population is small and relatively stable

What the Data Showed

Scientists analyzed outbreak data for three childhood diseases and found remarkable power-law patterns:

$$P(\text{epidemic} \geq s) = s^{-\alpha}$$

Measles: $\alpha \approx 0.265$

Whooping Cough (Pertussis): $\alpha \approx 0.255$

Mumps: $\alpha \approx 0.447$

This power-law relationship held for epidemics up to about 500-1,000 cases. For smaller outbreaks, the frequency decreased smoothly as a power function of size.

Connecting Models to Reality

Rhodes and Anderson modified the basic forest-fire cellular automaton model to better match human behavior. Their key innovation: allowing individuals to move.

The Modified Model

  • Individuals still sit on a lattice, but they can move to neighboring empty cells
  • Movement creates spatial mixing that's more realistic than fixed positions
  • The model can be run in different dimensions (2D, 3D, 5D) to represent different levels of social connectivity

What Do Different Dimensions Mean?

Higher dimensions don't literally mean physical space - they represent social connectivity:

  • 2D: Very local interactions (like a rural community where you only see nearby neighbors)
  • 3D: More connectivity (like a small town with schools, shops, community centers)
  • 5D: High connectivity (like a city with complex social networks)

Measles and whooping cough fit 3D models ($\alpha \approx 0.2$), while mumps fits 5D models ($\alpha \approx 0.4$), suggesting mumps spreads through more complex social networks.

Why This Matters

Practical Implications

  1. Predictability: Power laws tell us that while we can't predict the exact size of the next outbreak, we can predict the distribution of outbreak sizes over time.
  2. Resource Planning: Public health officials can plan for a mix of small, medium, and large outbreaks following the power-law pattern.
  3. Critical Thresholds: These models help identify vaccination thresholds needed to prevent large-scale epidemics.
  4. Early Warning: Understanding the power-law can help distinguish between natural variation and true changes in disease dynamics.

Step-by-Step: How the Analysis Works

  1. Collect Data: Gather historical outbreak sizes (number of cases in each epidemic)
  2. Sort by Size: Order outbreaks from smallest to largest
  3. Count Frequency: For each size $s$, count how many outbreaks were at least that large
  4. Plot on Log-Log Scale: Plot $\log(\text{frequency})$ vs $\log(\text{size})$
  5. Look for Straight Line: A straight line on log-log plot indicates power law, slope gives $\alpha$
  6. Compare to Model: Run cellular automaton simulations and check if they produce similar $\alpha$ values

Mathematical Note

Why does a straight line on a log-log plot indicate a power law?

If $P(\geq s) = s^{-\alpha}$, then:

$$\log(P) = \log(s^{-\alpha}) = -\alpha \times \log(s)$$

This is a straight line with slope $-\alpha$

Intuition and Analogies

The Apartment Building Analogy

Contact Process: Imagine a disease spreading through a single hallway of apartments. Each infected person can only infect their immediate neighbors (left and right). Once those neighbors are infected, they've used up their local supply of susceptibles. This is why the disease has a hard time persisting - it keeps running into dead ends.

Forest-Fire Model: Now imagine a whole city of apartment buildings. Fires start occasionally (lightning = imported cases). Fires spread to neighboring buildings (transmission). Burned buildings are torn down and left as empty lots (recovered/immune). Over years, new buildings are constructed in those lots (births or waning immunity). The city reaches a balance where there are always some buildings, some fires spreading, and some empty lots being rebuilt.

The Wildfire Analogy for Power Laws

Think about wildfires in a forest. Most fires are small - maybe just a few trees before the wind dies or rain comes. Some fires are medium-sized. Very few become massive forest fires. But this isn't random - the distribution of fire sizes follows a mathematical pattern (power law) that emerges from the basic rules of fire spread.

Similarly, disease outbreaks in the Faroe Islands showed the same pattern: lots of small outbreaks, fewer medium ones, rare large epidemics, all following a predictable mathematical relationship.

Limitations and When to Use These Models

Important Caveats

  • Abstract, Not Realistic: Real people don't sit on fixed grids. These models are tools for understanding principles, not for detailed predictions.
  • Limited Neighbors: Having only 4 or 8 neighbors is unrealistic for human diseases. Real social networks are much more complex.
  • Best Use: Understanding how spatial structure and local depletion affect epidemic dynamics.
  • When to Choose Another Model: For detailed predictions about specific populations, use individual-based models or network models instead.

Summary

Cellular automata models place individuals on a grid and apply simple local rules for disease transmission. The contact process (SIS model) demonstrates how spatial structure increases the threshold for disease persistence due to local depletion of susceptibles. The forest-fire model (SIRS model) produces power-law distributions of epidemic sizes that match real childhood disease data when transmission is much faster than recovery, which is faster than births, which is faster than imports. While these models are abstract, they reveal fundamental principles about how spatial structure shapes epidemic dynamics, particularly the importance of local mixing and the emergence of scale-free outbreak patterns.
