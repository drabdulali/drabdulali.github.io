Understanding Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD)

Foot-and-Mouth Disease is a highly contagious viral disease affecting livestock. This simulator helps you understand how spatial distance affects disease transmission between farms.

Key Concept: Not all farms have equal risk of infection. Farms closer to infected premises face much higher risk than distant farms. The spatial kernel mathematically describes this relationship.

How the Simulation Works

Overview

This interactive simulator demonstrates the mathematical principles behind Foot-and-Mouth Disease transmission using a spatial modeling approach. The simulation implements the key concepts from Keeling & Rohani's "Modeling Infectious Diseases in Humans and Animals," allowing you to visualize how disease spreads between farms based on distance-dependent transmission.

Core Mathematical Framework

The simulation operates in three main steps each day:

Step 1: Calculate Infection Pressure

For each susceptible farm, we calculate the "force of infection" \(\lambda_i\) which represents the total infectious pressure it faces from all nearby infected farms. This is given by:

\[ \lambda_i = \text{Sus}_i \times \sum_{j \in \text{infectious}} \left(\text{Trans}_j \times K(d_{ij})\right) \]

Where the spatial kernel \(K(d)\) decreases exponentially with distance:

\[ K(d) = K_0 \times \exp\left(-\frac{d}{r_0}\right) \]
  • \(\text{Sus}_i\) = Number of susceptible animals on farm \(i\) (more animals = bigger target)
  • \(\text{Trans}_j\) = Transmission strength from infected farm \(j\) (more infected animals = more virus)
  • \(K(d_{ij})\) = Kernel value at distance \(d_{ij}\) between farms \(i\) and \(j\)
  • \(K_0\) = Baseline transmission rate at distance zero
  • \(r_0\) = Characteristic distance scale (controls how quickly transmission decreases)

Step 2: Determine New Infections

The probability that farm \(i\) becomes infected on a given day is:

\[ P(\text{farm } i \text{ infected}) = 1 - \exp(-\lambda_i) \]

This exponential transformation ensures probabilities stay between 0 and 1, even when \(\lambda_i\) is large. The simulator uses this probability to randomly determine which susceptible farms become newly infected.

Step 3: Update Disease States

Infected farms progress through disease states over time:

  • Days 0-5: Exposed (E) - Farm is infected but not yet shedding virus. Animals appear healthy but virus is replicating internally.
  • Days 5-9: Infectious (I) - Farm is actively shedding virus and can infect others, but symptoms may not yet be visible. This is the dangerous "silent transmission" period.
  • Day 9+: Symptomatic (S) - Visible clinical signs appear (lesions, lameness, reduced milk production). Farm can be detected and control measures implemented.

Control Strategies (Tab 3)

The simulation implements three control strategies used during the 2001 UK FMD outbreak:

1. IP (Infected Premises) Culling: When a farm becomes symptomatic (day 9+), it is detected and all animals are culled within 1 day. This removes the source but may be too slow to prevent spread.

2. DC (Dangerous Contact) Culling: Farms with high infection pressure (\(\lambda_i > 10\)) are identified as dangerous contacts and preemptively culled within 2 days. This targets farms likely to have been exposed through shared equipment, personnel, or recent animal movements.

3. CP (Contiguous Premises) Culling: All farms within a specified radius (default 2 km) of a symptomatic farm are culled within 2 days. This addresses local transmission across boundaries, shared water sources, and aerosol spread.

You can enable/disable these strategies and compare outcomes - the simulation will show how combining multiple approaches leads to faster epidemic control with fewer total farms affected.

Simulation Scale

Spatial Scale: In the simulation, 10 pixels = 1 kilometer. This means farms that appear close together on screen may actually be separated by several kilometers in reality.

Key Insights You Can Explore

  • Distance matters: Adjust \(r_0\) to see how transmission range affects epidemic spread. Small \(r_0\) = localized outbreaks. Large \(r_0\) = widespread transmission.
  • Silent transmission is dangerous: Farms spread disease for 4 days before symptoms appear. By the time you detect an outbreak, it's already spreading!
  • Proactive control is essential: Compare simulations with only IP culling vs. IP+DC+CP. You'll see why "hit hard, hit early" was critical in 2001.
  • Exponential growth: Without control, epidemics grow exponentially. Even small delays in implementing control measures lead to dramatically larger outbreaks.
  • Spatial clustering: Infections tend to cluster spatially, forming local hotspots that then seed new clusters elsewhere.
Important Note: This is a simplified educational model. Real FMD outbreaks involve additional complexities including multiple livestock species with different susceptibilities, wind-borne transmission, variation in farm sizes, and logistical constraints on control measures. However, the fundamental principles of distance-dependent transmission and the mathematics of spatial kernels remain central to understanding disease spread.

The Spatial Transmission Kernel

\[ K(d) = K_0 \times \exp\left(-\frac{d}{r_0}\right) \]
What does each part mean?
  • \(K(d)\) = Transmission strength at distance \(d\)
  • \(K_0\) = Maximum transmission rate (at distance 0)
  • \(d\) = Distance between farms (in km)
  • \(r_0\) = Characteristic distance (how quickly transmission decreases)
  • exp = Exponential function \((e^x)\)
Higher values = stronger baseline transmission
Higher values = disease spreads further

Control Strategies: IP, DC, and CP Culling

Real-world context: During the 2001 UK FMD outbreak, over 6 million animals were culled to control the epidemic. Understanding which control strategies work best is crucial for minimizing both disease spread and economic losses.

Three Types of Culling:

1. IP (Infected Premises) Culling

All animals on confirmed infected farms are culled. This removes the source of infection but may be too slow if disease has already spread.

2. DC (Dangerous Contact) Culling

Farms that had risky contact with infected farms (shared equipment, personnel, recent animal purchases) are identified and culled preemptively.

3. CP (Contiguous Premises) Culling

Farms that share physical boundaries with infected farms are culled because of high risk of direct transmission across fences.

The Mathematics Behind the Simulation

Force of Infection (λᵢ)

\[ \lambda_i = \text{Sus}_i \times \sum_{j \in \text{infectious}} \left(\text{Trans}_j \times K(d_{ij})\right) \]
Breaking it down:
  • λᵢ (lambda) = Infection rate for farm i
  • Susᵢ = Number of susceptible animals on farm i
  • Transⱼ = Transmission contribution from infected farm j
  • K(dᵢⱼ) = Kernel value at distance between farms i and j
  • Σ (sigma) = Sum over all infected farms

In plain English: The infection pressure on a farm equals its vulnerability (number of animals) multiplied by the sum of threats from all infected farms, where each threat is weighted by distance.

Infection Probability

\[ P(\text{infection}) = 1 - \exp(-\lambda_i) \]
Why this formula? This exponential transformation ensures:
  • Probability stays between 0 and 1 (never exceeds 100%)
  • When λ is small, P ≈ λ (approximately linear)
  • When λ is large, P approaches 1 (saturation)
  • Matches Poisson process theory for rare events

Disease Progression Timeline

Days 0-5: Exposed (E) - Virus replicating, not yet infectious
Days 5-9: Infectious (I) - Can transmit to other farms, may look normal
Day 9+: Symptomatic (S) - Visible lesions, detection possible

Critical insight: Farms are infectious BEFORE symptoms appear, allowing "silent spread"!

Real-World Application: 2001 UK FMD Outbreak

Timeline

  • February 20, 2001: First case reported
  • First 5 weeks: Over 600 farms infected
  • Peak: Exponential growth phase
  • October 2001: Epidemic declared over
  • Final toll: 2,000+ infected, 8,000+ culled

Key Lessons

  • Speed matters: Days of delay = thousands more farms lost
  • Proactive culling essential: Can't just react
  • Spatial structure crucial: Localized clusters form
  • Mathematical models guided policy in real-time
Impact of Delay: With exponential growth rate r ≈ 0.2 per day, a 10-day delay means the epidemic grows by a factor of exp(0.2 × 10) = 7.4! This explains why "hit hard, hit early" was so critical.
