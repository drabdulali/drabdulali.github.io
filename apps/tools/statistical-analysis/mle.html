How Maximum Likelihood Works

MLE finds the parameter values that maximize the probability of observing the data we have.

1

Collect Data

Observe a sample of \(n\) data points \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\) from an unknown distribution.

2

Choose a Model

Assume a probability distribution \(f(x|\theta)\) with unknown parameter \(\theta\).

3

Write the Likelihood

Compute \(L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i|\theta)\), the joint probability of all data.

4

Maximize

Find \(\hat{\theta}_{MLE} = \arg\max_\theta \ln L(\theta)\) by setting the score to zero.

Mathematical Framework

General Maximum Likelihood Theory

Likelihood Function:

$$L(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i \mid \theta)$$

Log-Likelihood (easier to optimize):

$$\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln f(x_i \mid \theta)$$

Score Function & MLE condition:

$$U(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \hat{\theta}_{MLE}$$

Fisher Information & Asymptotic Variance:

$$I(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\right], \qquad \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \approx \frac{1}{I(\theta)}$$

As \(n \to \infty\): \(\hat{\theta}_{MLE} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\theta_0,\; \frac{1}{nI(\theta_0)}\right)\)

\(L(\theta|\mathbf{x})\)
Likelihood: probability of data as a function of the parameter
\(\ell(\theta)\)
Log-likelihood: log of the likelihood (same maximizer)
\(U(\theta)\)
Score function: gradient of the log-likelihood
\(I(\theta)\)
Fisher information: expected curvature of the log-likelihood
\(\hat{\theta}_{MLE}\)
MLE estimate: value of \(\theta\) that maximizes the likelihood
\(\text{SE} = 1/\sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}\)
Standard error: estimated uncertainty of the MLE

Distribution-Specific MLE Solutions

Normal: \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\)

$$\hat{\mu}_{MLE} = \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2_{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

Poisson: \(X \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda)\)

$$\hat{\lambda}_{MLE} = \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$$

Binomial: \(X \sim \text{Bin}(m, p)\)  (known \(m\))

$$\hat{p}_{MLE} = \frac{\bar{x}}{m} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n \cdot m}$$

Exponential: \(X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)\)

$$\hat{\lambda}_{MLE} = \frac{1}{\bar{x}} = \frac{n}{\sum x_i}$$

Properties of MLE

Consistency
\(\hat\theta_{MLE} \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\) as \(n \to \infty\). The estimate converges to the true value.
Asymptotic Normality
\(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, I(\theta_0)^{-1})\). Becomes normally distributed for large \(n\).
Efficiency
Achieves the Cramer-Rao lower bound asymptotically: lowest possible variance among unbiased estimators.
Invariance
If \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of \(\theta\), then \(g(\hat\theta)\) is the MLE of \(g(\theta)\) for any function \(g\).

Key Insight: Why MLE is Fundamental in Statistics

Maximum Likelihood Estimation is the backbone of modern statistical inference. It provides a principled, general-purpose recipe for parameter estimation that works with any parametric model. Its deep connection to information theory (via Fisher information and the Cramer-Rao bound) guarantees that MLEs are the most efficient estimators possible for large samples. In ecology, MLE underpins species distribution models, mark-recapture population estimates, survival analysis, and virtually every regression model. The sampling distribution visualization above demonstrates the Central Limit Theorem in action — as you increase the sample size, the MLE estimates become more tightly concentrated around the true parameter value.
