4 Likelihood Construction

Likelihood Construction

Likelihood is the scoring function that tells the optimizer how well any given \((\beta,\gamma)\) pair explains the observed data. Choosing the right distribution for that score is critical — get it wrong and all your parameter estimates will be biased.

What is likelihood — in plain English?

Suppose your model predicts that 10 farms should be reported sick today. In reality, 14 are reported. The likelihood asks: if the model is correct and the true expected count is 10, how probable is it to observe exactly 14? That probability is the likelihood for that one day. The total likelihood is the product of these probabilities across all 50 days of the outbreak.

The optimizer (Step 6) tries many different values of \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). For each pair, it runs the ODE to get predicted counts, scores them using likelihood, and searches for the pair that makes the data most probable. The distribution you choose for the likelihood determines what "probable" means — which is why choosing the right one matters so much.

What is \(k\) on the x-axis? What is \(P(X=k)\) on the y-axis?

This is the most common source of confusion in Step 4, so let us be very explicit.

ⓘ

\(k\) = a possible count value, NOT a day number

The x-axis shows different possible outcomes: \(k=0\) means "zero farms reported sick on this day", \(k=5\) means "five farms reported sick", and so on. The y-axis shows \(P(X=k)\) — the probability that on a randomly picked day from the outbreak, exactly \(k\) farms were reported. The bars show what actually happened in your data; the lines show what each distribution predicts should have happened.

Three candidate distributions — which fits outbreak data best?

Poisson

Assumes the variance in case counts equals the mean. Works well for very uniform processes. Breaks down for outbreaks, which are inherently clumpy — some days have many more cases than others.

Negative Binomial

Allows variance to exceed the mean (overdispersion). Has an extra parameter \(k\) that controls clumping. Small \(k\) = very clumpy data (a few superspreader days dominate). Large \(k \to \infty\) = converges to Poisson.

Binomial

Assumes you know the exact number of farms at risk each day (a fixed \(N\)). Appropriate for household studies or vaccine trials. Rarely the right choice for farm outbreaks where the susceptible pool changes continuously.

For farm outbreaks, Negative Binomial almost always wins. Real outbreaks are clumpy: one infected market farm might expose 30 others; most infected farms expose only 1 or 2. This creates far more variance than Poisson can accommodate. The dispersion parameter \(k\) captures exactly this heterogeneity.

Grey bars = observed frequency of each count value across all days of the outbreak. Coloured lines = what each distribution predicts. The distribution whose line sits closest to the tops of the bars is the best fit.
What to look for: The Negative Binomial (red) hugs the bars most closely, especially at \(k=0\) (many days with very few cases) and at higher \(k\) values (occasional large spikes). Poisson (blue) consistently underestimates the frequency of both extremes — it predicts data that is too regular and lacks the real-world clumping seen in outbreak surveillance.

The Negative Binomial formula — what each part means

Before the formula: The NegBin distribution describes the probability of observing \(y_t\) cases on day \(t\), given that the model predicts a mean of \(\mu_t\) cases and that there is clumping described by dispersion parameter \(k\). Think of it as a generalised Poisson that allows for extra variability.
Negative Binomial probability mass function
\[P(Y_t = y_t \mid \mu_t,\, k) = \binom{y_t + k - 1}{y_t}\left(\frac{k}{k+\mu_t}\right)^{\!k}\left(\frac{\mu_t}{k+\mu_t}\right)^{\!y_t}\]
  • \(y_t\) = observed case count on day \(t\) (from your cleaned data)
  • \(\mu_t = \hat{I}_t(\beta,\gamma)\) = predicted count from the SIR ODE for that \((\beta,\gamma)\) pair
  • \(k\) = dispersion parameter — estimated alongside \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). Smaller \(k\) = more clumping
  • The binomial coefficient \(\binom{y_t+k-1}{y_t}\) counts the number of ways the outcome could arise
  • As \(k \to \infty\), the formula converges to the Poisson distribution

The full log-likelihood — summing across all days

Why sum logarithms instead of multiplying probabilities? The total likelihood is the product of all daily probabilities: \(L = P(y_1) \times P(y_2) \times \cdots \times P(y_{50})\). Each probability might be 0.05 or smaller. Multiplying 50 numbers smaller than 0.1 gives something like \(10^{-50}\) — too small for computers to represent precisely. Taking the log converts multiplication to addition, and addition is numerically stable. The log-likelihood \(\ell\) is the quantity that the optimizer actually works with.
Full log-likelihood — summed over all \(T\) days
\[\ell(\beta,\gamma,k) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P_{\text{NB}}\!\left(y_t \,\Big|\, \mu_t(\beta,\gamma),\, k\right)\]
  • \(\ell\) (script "ell") = the log-likelihood — a single number that scores any \((\beta,\gamma,k)\) combination
  • \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\) = sum over all days \(t\) from day 1 to day \(T=50\)
  • Higher \(\ell\) = better fit. The MLE finds the \((\beta,\gamma,k)\) that maximises \(\ell\)
  • In practice we minimise \(-\ell\) (Step 6), because optimisers are built to go downhill

V12 — Q-Q plots: does each distribution fit the tails?

A Q-Q (quantile-quantile) plot compares what a distribution predicts to what actually happened, across the full range of possible counts — not just the average. If the distribution fits perfectly, all points fall on the diagonal line. Points that curve away from the diagonal reveal systematic misfits at the low or high end.

Poisson Q-Q plot — points curve away
Poisson underestimates the frequency of very high count days — its tail is too thin. Points curve above the diagonal at the right.
Negative Binomial Q-Q plot — points near diagonal ✓
NegBin points sit close to the diagonal across the full range — including the heavy right tail of occasional large reporting days.

V13 — AIC model comparison

AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) is a formal score that rewards a good fit but penalises models that achieve it by adding more parameters. Lower AIC = better. A difference of more than 10 between two models is considered decisive evidence in favour of the lower one.

AIC formula
\[\text{AIC} = -2\,\ell + 2p\]
\(\ell\) = log-likelihood at the best-fit parameters; \(p\) = number of free parameters. Negative Binomial has \(p=2\) (\(\mu\) and \(k\)); Poisson has \(p=1\) (just \(\mu\)); Binomial has \(p=2\) (\(n\) and \(\pi\)). Despite NegBin using one more parameter than Poisson, its dramatic improvement in fit more than compensates.

V14 — Log-likelihood shape: finding the MLE and its uncertainty

The profile likelihood curve for a single parameter (fixing all others at their best values) tells you two things: where the peak is (that is the MLE estimate) and how wide the peak is (that determines the confidence interval). A sharp narrow peak means the data pins down that parameter precisely. A wide flat peak means the data is consistent with many different values — you have high uncertainty.

How the 95% CI is read from the curve: The \(\chi^2\) distribution tells us that the 95% confidence interval spans all parameter values where the profile log-likelihood is within 1.92 units of its maximum. On the plot, draw a horizontal dashed line at \(\ell_{\max} - 1.92\). Where this line intersects the curve on the left and right — those are your CI boundaries.
Profile log-likelihood for \(\beta\) — red = MLE, amber = 95% CI
The red vertical line marks the MLE (\(\hat\beta = 0.320\)). The two amber lines mark the CI boundaries (\([0.278,\; 0.368]\)). The amber shaded region is the full 95% CI. The grey dashed horizontal line is the \(\chi^2\) cutoff at \(\ell_{\max} - 1.92\).
Profile log-likelihood for \(\gamma\) — red = MLE, amber = 95% CI
\(\gamma\) has a sharper, narrower curve than \(\beta\), meaning it is estimated more precisely from this data. CI: \([0.089,\; 0.134]\). Width of \(\beta\) CI = 0.090; width of \(\gamma\) CI = 0.045 — \(\gamma\) is roughly twice as precisely estimated.
Profile likelihood 95% confidence interval
\[\mathcal{C}_{95} = \left\{\theta : \ell_p(\theta) \geq \ell_{\max} - \tfrac{1}{2}\chi^2_{1,\,0.95}\right\} = \left\{\theta : \ell_p(\theta) \geq \ell_{\max} - 1.92\right\}\]
  • \(\ell_p(\theta)\) = the profile log-likelihood at parameter value \(\theta\) (with all other parameters optimised out)
  • \(\ell_{\max}\) = the maximum log-likelihood (at the MLE)
  • \(\chi^2_{1,\,0.95} = 3.84\), so \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.84 = 1.92\) — this is the 1.92 unit drop
  • The CI contains every \(\theta\) value that is "statistically plausible" given the data — those that make the data nearly as probable as the MLE does

Overdispersion test — confirming NegBin is needed

Before accepting NegBin over Poisson, it is good practice to formally test whether the data is overdispersed. The simplest check: if variance / mean ≈ 1, Poisson may suffice. If variance / mean ≫ 1, NegBin is needed.

What Step 4 delivers to the pipeline

A confirmed scoring function: the Negative Binomial log-likelihood with dispersion \(\hat{k} = 1.52\). From Step 5 onwards, every \((\beta,\gamma)\) candidate is scored using this function. The optimizer in Step 6 maximises it. The confidence intervals in Step 8 are built from its profile. Everything downstream depends on this choice being correct.
