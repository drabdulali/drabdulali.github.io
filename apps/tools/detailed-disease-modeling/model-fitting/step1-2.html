12 Data Generation & Model Formulation

Data Generation & the SIR Model

Before fitting a model to real data, we practice on simulated data where the truth is already known. We also introduce the SIR model — the mathematical engine that the rest of the pipeline is built around.

← Overview 1–2 Data & model 3 Preprocessing 4 Likelihood 5 Grid search 6 MLE 7 Goodness of fit 8 Confidence intervals 9 Validation 10–11 LHS & sensitivity

Step 1 — Why simulate data first?

Imagine you are a detective. Before investigating a real crime scene, you practice on a staged one where you already know what happened. If your methods correctly reconstruct the staged crime, you gain confidence they will work on the real one.

Data simulation works exactly the same way. We create a fake outbreak where we control the true values of every parameter — transmission rate, recovery rate, farm locations, herd sizes — and then run the entire pipeline to see whether it recovers those true values. If it does, we trust it on real data. If it does not, we fix the method before touching any real data.

ⓘ

What "simulation-based validation" means

Running the full 11-step pipeline on data where you know the truth, and checking whether your estimates come close to those true values. This is also what Step 9 (validation) does formally — but here we use it to build confidence before any real fitting begins.

How the farms and outbreak are simulated

Farm sizes vary greatly in the real world — most farms are small but a few are very large. We capture this with a log-normal distribution, which produces exactly this kind of skewed pattern.

In plain English: If you took the log of every farm's herd size, the result would look like a bell curve. This means a few farms are 10 or 20 times bigger than the average — just like in real agriculture.
Farm herd size distribution
\[H_i \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu_H,\, \sigma_H^2), \quad i = 1, \ldots, 200\]
Reading this equation:
  • \(H_i\) = the herd size of farm number \(i\)
  • \(\sim\) means "is drawn from" (sampled from that distribution)
  • \(\mu_H\) and \(\sigma_H^2\) control the average and spread of log-farm-sizes

How disease spreads between farms: farms that are closer together are more likely to infect each other. We model this with a Gaussian kernel — a mathematical function that starts at 1 when two farms are on top of each other and drops smoothly toward 0 as they get further apart.

In plain English: Think of each infected farm as casting a "cloud" of infection risk around itself. The cloud is dense nearby and fades with distance. Neighbouring farms are almost certain to be exposed; distant farms are barely at risk at all.
Distance-dependent transmission kernel
\[K(d_{ij}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{2\sigma_K^2}\right)\]
Reading this equation:
  • \(d_{ij}\) = the distance between farm \(i\) and farm \(j\)
  • \(\sigma_K\) = how quickly risk fades with distance (the "reach" of the infection cloud)
  • When \(d_{ij} = 0\) (farms are adjacent), \(K = 1\) (maximum risk)
  • When distance is large, the exponential drives \(K\) close to zero (minimal risk)

Finally, a realistic observation layer is applied: not every case is reported immediately. Each true new infection is detected with probability 0.75 and recorded with a random delay of 0–3 days. This creates the noisy, slightly-delayed epidemic curve that the pipeline actually sees — mimicking what a real surveillance system would produce.

Step 2 — The SIR model: three boxes and two arrows

The SIR model divides every farm in the population into one of three groups — or "compartments" — at any point in time. Think of it as sorting every farm into one of three boxes:

S — Susceptible

Healthy farms that could catch the disease. They have never been infected and have no immunity. This is where the epidemic "fuel" comes from.

I — Infected

Farms currently infected and spreading disease to susceptible neighbours. The size of this group determines how fast the epidemic grows.

R — Recovered

Farms that cleared the infection. They are now immune and cannot be reinfected. As this group grows, the epidemic slows and eventually stops.

At any moment, every farm is in exactly one box. Farms can only move in one direction: S → I → R. They cannot go backwards. And the total number of farms \(S + I + R = N = 200\) never changes.

The SIR equations — what they say in plain English

The SIR model is written as three differential equations. Each equation says how fast one compartment is changing at any instant. Do not be intimidated — each equation has a simple meaning.

Before the equations — what \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) mean physically:

\(\beta\) (transmission rate) = how fast an infected farm infects others per day. High \(\beta\) = fast spread. Low \(\beta\) = slow spread.

\(\gamma\) (recovery rate) = the fraction of infected farms that recover each day. If \(\gamma = 0.11\), then about 11% of infected farms recover every day, meaning the average farm stays infectious for about 9 days (\(1/0.11 \approx 9\)).
SIR differential equations
\[\frac{dS}{dt} = -\frac{\beta\, S\, I}{N}\] \[\frac{dI}{dt} = +\frac{\beta\, S\, I}{N} - \gamma\, I\] \[\frac{dR}{dt} = +\gamma\, I\]
Reading each equation line by line:
  • \(dS/dt\) = how fast S is changing (negative = S is shrinking as farms get infected)
  • \(dI/dt\) = how fast I is changing (gains from new infections, loses to recoveries)
  • \(dR/dt\) = how fast R is changing (only gains, as farms recover)
  • \(\beta S I / N\) = the "force of infection" — how many new infections happen per day. It depends on how many susceptible farms exist (\(S\)), how many infectious farms exist (\(I\)), and is divided by \(N\) to make it "per farm" (frequency-dependent)
  • \(\gamma I\) = how many infected farms recover per day
ⓘ

Why divide by N? — Frequency-dependent transmission explained

Dividing by N means risk depends on the fraction of farms that are infected, not the absolute number. This is realistic for farm networks: if you are an isolated farm in a remote corner, your risk does not increase just because the total farm population doubled somewhere else. Your risk depends on what fraction of farms near you are infected — not how many farms exist nationally.

The basic reproduction number \(\mathcal{R}_0\)

\(\mathcal{R}_0\) (pronounced "R-naught") is the single most important number in epidemic modelling. It answers one question: if one farm gets infected in a fully susceptible population, how many other farms will it infect on average before recovering?

In plain English: \(\mathcal{R}_0\) is the "branching factor" of the outbreak. If \(\mathcal{R}_0 = 2.91\), every infected farm infects about 3 others before it recovers. Those 3 each infect 3 more, and so on — the outbreak explodes. If \(\mathcal{R}_0 = 0.5\), each farm infects only half a farm on average, so the chain of transmission quickly dies out.
Basic reproduction number
\[\mathcal{R}_0 = \frac{\beta}{\gamma}\]
Where does this formula come from?
  • An infected farm is infectious for \(1/\gamma\) days on average (the mean infectious period)
  • Each day it infects new farms at rate \(\beta\) (when surrounded by fully susceptible farms)
  • So total infections caused = \(\beta \times (1/\gamma) = \beta/\gamma\)
  • With our true values: \(\mathcal{R}_0 = 0.32 / 0.11 = 2.91\)
Epidemic threshold and herd immunity
\[\mathcal{R}_0 > 1 \Rightarrow \text{epidemic grows} \qquad \mathcal{R}_0 < 1 \Rightarrow \text{epidemic fades out}\] \[p_c = 1 - \frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_0} = 1 - \frac{1}{2.91} \approx 65.7\%\]
\(p_c\) = the herd immunity threshold: the fraction of farms that need to be immune (through vaccination or prior infection) to prevent an outbreak. With \(\mathcal{R}_0 = 2.91\), about 66% of farms must be immune before a new introduction cannot spread. Below this threshold, the outbreak grows. Above it, each infected farm infects less than one new farm on average and the chain breaks.

The true epidemic (red dashed) vs the noisy reported counts (blue bars) after applying the observation layer: 75% detection probability + 0–3 day reporting delay.
Why this gap matters: The pipeline never sees the true red curve. It only ever works with the blue bars — incomplete, delayed, and noisy. The entire job of the likelihood function (Step 4) is to account for this observation uncertainty when estimating \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\).

Permanent immunity after recoveryYesNo reinfection — once R, always R. No waning immunity.

What Steps 1–2 give the rest of the pipeline

Two things only: (1) a noisy observed epidemic curve — the blue bars in V3 — which every subsequent step treats as the "data", and (2) a clear target — the true values \(\beta = 0.32\), \(\gamma = 0.11\), \(\mathcal{R}_0 = 2.91\). The pipeline's job is to recover these values from the noisy data alone, without ever peeking at the true curve.
