3 Data Preprocessing

Data Preprocessing

Real outbreak data is messy, delayed, and incomplete. Preprocessing cleans and prepares it so the likelihood function in Step 4 fits the true biology — not reporting artefacts. We also extract a quick preliminary estimate of \(\mathcal{R}_0\) before any formal fitting.

Why does preprocessing matter?

Imagine you are trying to estimate how many cars pass through a city intersection each hour. But your counter is broken — it misses 25% of cars, and some counts are recorded 30 minutes late. If you fit a model to these raw counts without correcting for these problems, your estimate will be biased. Preprocessing fixes the measurement problems before the model sees the data.

The same applies to outbreak data. Farms are not always reported the day they are found. Some outbreaks go undetected entirely. Labs have backlogs. Weekends have fewer reports than weekdays. None of this reflects real biology — it reflects the imperfect surveillance system. Preprocessing separates the biological signal from the reporting noise.

The 12-item preprocessing checklist

  • Data inspection: Check the format (line list vs daily aggregated counts), the date range, what each row represents (farm / animal / herd), and scan for duplicate records that would double-count cases.
  • Date standardisation: Convert all dates to a single format (YYYY-MM-DD). Create an "epidemic day" variable where Day 1 = date of the first confirmed case. This makes all subsequent calculations consistent.
  • Fill missing dates: If no cases were reported on a given date, insert a row with count = 0. A missing row is ambiguous — it could mean zero cases or missing data. Explicit zeros remove that ambiguity.
  • Handle missing attributes: For farms where herd size, GPS location, or farm type is not recorded — decide whether to impute (fill with mean/median), drop the record, or flag it. Document every decision and why.
  • Outlier detection: Plot the raw time series. Flag any day where the case count exceeds 3 standard deviations above the 7-day rolling mean. These spikes often reflect laboratory backlogs being cleared in one batch, not a real biological surge.
  • 7-day rolling average: Smooth the daily counts to remove the regular dip that appears every weekend (fewer staff = fewer reports). Both the raw series and the smooth series are kept — raw for likelihood fitting, smooth for visualisations.
  • Reporting delay correction (nowcasting): The most recent 1–3 weeks of data are almost always under-counted because some cases have not yet been reported. Estimate the delay distribution from the line list (time from infection to report date) and adjust recent counts upward.
  • Case definition check: Was the definition of a "confirmed case" changed during the outbreak? If the definition was broadened midway, case counts will jump artificially on that date. This is not biology — split the time series at that date or adjust counts to a consistent definition.
  • Attach population denominators: Each farm needs its herd size \(N_i\) attached to it. This is the denominator in \(\beta SI/N\) and must be correct for the model to produce meaningful probabilities.
  • Aggregate and reshape: If data is a line list, aggregate to daily incidence counts. Verify that the sum of aggregated daily counts exactly matches the total number of records in the line list — a mismatch reveals a data processing error.
  • Identify the early exponential phase: Mark the period before the epidemic peak — usually the first 1–3 weeks — as the "early phase". This window is used to estimate the growth rate \(r\) in the next section.
  • Final integrity check: Plot the cleaned epidemic curve. Confirm total case count matches. Save the cleaned dataset as a separate file — never overwrite the original raw data. Write a change log documenting every transformation and the reason for it.

Estimating the growth rate \(r\)

During the first few weeks of any outbreak — before the epidemic has used up many susceptible individuals — the number of new cases grows exponentially. This means it roughly doubles every few days. Exponential growth has a very useful mathematical property: if you take the logarithm of the case counts, the curve becomes a straight line. And the slope of that line is the growth rate \(r\).

In plain English — what exponential growth looks like:

Day 1: 2 cases. Day 4: 4 cases. Day 7: 8 cases. Day 10: 16 cases.

On a regular scale this is a hockey-stick curve curving upward. On a log scale (where the y-axis is log(cases) instead of cases) the exact same data becomes a straight line. The slope of that line tells you how fast the outbreak is doubling.
Exponential growth model (early phase)
\[I(t) = I_0 \cdot e^{\,r\,t}\]
  • \(I(t)\) = number of infected farms at day \(t\)
  • \(I_0\) = number at day zero (start of outbreak)
  • \(r\) = growth rate — the number we want to estimate
  • \(e\) = Euler's number (≈ 2.718) — the base of natural logarithms

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides transforms this into a straight line:

Log-linear form — taking ln of both sides
\[\ln I(t) = \ln I_0 + r \cdot t\]
This is exactly the equation of a straight line: \(y = \text{intercept} + \text{slope} \times x\). Here \(y = \ln I(t)\), \(x = t\) (day number), the intercept is \(\ln I_0\), and the slope is \(r\). So to find \(r\), we just fit a straight line to the log-transformed early-phase case counts and read off the slope.
V9 — Log-linear fit to the early epidemic phase
Blue dots = \(\ln(\text{daily cases})\) for each day in the early phase. Red line = ordinary least-squares regression line. The slope of the red line is the estimated growth rate \(\hat{r}\). Grey dots = late-phase data excluded from this fit (the relationship is no longer linear after the peak).
What to look for: Blue dots should form a reasonably straight line. If they curve upward or downward, either the early phase identification is wrong or the outbreak is not purely exponential (perhaps because spatial depletion of susceptibles is already slowing spread). The slope estimate printed in the legend is your \(\hat{r}\).

Crude \(\mathcal{R}_0\) — a quick estimate before formal fitting

Once we have \(\hat{r}\) from the log-linear fit, we can get a rough estimate of \(\mathcal{R}_0\) without running any optimiser. This is useful as a sanity check — if the crude estimate comes out at 15, something is probably wrong with either the data or the assumed \(\gamma\).

The logic behind the crude formula:

In the very early outbreak, almost everyone is susceptible (so \(S \approx N\)). Under this assumption the SIR equations simplify dramatically. The infected compartment grows at rate \(r = \beta - \gamma\). Rearranging: \(\beta = r + \gamma\), and since \(\mathcal{R}_0 = \beta/\gamma\), substituting gives \(\mathcal{R}_0 = r/\gamma + 1\). This is the crude formula.
Crude \(\mathcal{R}_0\) from growth rate
\[\mathcal{R}_0 \approx \frac{\hat{r}}{\gamma} + 1\]
  • \(\hat{r}\) = slope of the log-linear fit from V9 (estimated from data)
  • \(\gamma\) = assumed recovery rate — taken from literature or veterinary knowledge
  • The result is called "crude" because it assumes full susceptibility (true only at day 1) and ignores spatial structure, overdispersion, and reporting delays
  • With our simulated data: \(\hat{r} \approx 0.21\), \(\gamma = 0.11\) → \(\mathcal{R}_0 \approx 0.21/0.11 + 1 = 2.91\) ✓

⚠

Why is this called a "crude" estimate — and when is it useful?

It is crude because it assumes the full population is susceptible (only true at the very start), it ignores spatial heterogeneity, and it requires \(\gamma\) to be assumed rather than estimated. Despite these limitations, it is extremely useful as a quick first look: it tells you the rough magnitude of \(\mathcal{R}_0\), suggests plausible ranges for \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) to search over in Step 5, and flags obvious data problems (if the crude estimate comes out negative or above 20, something is wrong).

What Step 3 hands to the rest of the pipeline

A clean daily case count time series (the cleaned \(y_t\) values) — ready for likelihood scoring in Step 4. A rough starting region in \((\beta, \gamma)\) space — the crude \(\mathcal{R}_0 \approx 2.91\) tells the grid search in Step 5 where to concentrate its search. And a documented record of every data decision made, for reproducibility.
